CHAPTER THIRTY-EIGHT
NICO BROADCASTS THE IMPOSSIBLE

Nico’s interview conditions had seven clauses. No preapproved questions. No government edit. No Visitor edit. No live audience.

Full recording released simultaneously to multiple independent archives. Raw translation layer preserved.

And if Shaal-Amur refused a question, the refusal stayed in the program. Pike read the list.

“You forgot no assassination.”

“That is implied.”

“It should never be implied.”

Shaal-Amur accepted all seven. That bothered Nico.

People who accepted dangerous interviews too easily usually believed they had nothing to lose.

They sat across from each other in a neutral studio built inside a conference facility outside Geneva. No dramatic lighting. No flags. No archive symbols.

Nico wanted the impossible to look ordinary enough that viewers had nowhere to hide from it. The cameras rolled.

“People are still calling you a projection, a deepfake, a government puppet, and - my favorite - an Antarctic weather balloon with excellent posture.” Nico glanced at his notes. “Do you care?”

Shaal-Amur answered, “Good.” Nico blinked.

“Good?”

“Doubt protects you from authority.”

“That is inconveniently charming.”

“I have been told charm is a governance hazard.”

Nico almost smiled. He started with evidence. Origin. Biology. History. The limits of Visitor claims.

Shaal-Amur repeatedly distinguished what he knew from what his civilization believed, which made every extraordinary statement harder to dismiss. Then Nico asked about gods.

“Did you ever claim divinity?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“To stop a war.”

“Did it work?”

“For nine years.”

“What happened in year ten?”

“A war.”

Nico let the silence sit.

“Did you ever enjoy worship?”

Shaal-Amur looked away for the first time.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because adoration resembles love to those who do not yet understand the difference.”

Nico felt the room disappear around that sentence.

“Did humans teach you the difference?”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

Shaal-Amur did not answer. Nico waited. The refusal stayed. They moved to the resets.

Nico did not call them cleansings. He used numbers. Population estimates. Destroyed settlements. Interrupted technologies. Cultural losses. Shaal-Amur did not deny them.

“Do you regret it?” Nico asked.

“Every day.”

“Would you do it again?”

“Yes.”

The answer landed like metal.

“Then what does regret mean?”

“That pain does not prove the alternative was better.”

“Convenient.”

“No.” Shaal-Amur’s voice hardened. “Convenience would be believing grief absolves me.”

Nico leaned forward.

“You keep saying survival. Survival of what?”

“Continuity of intelligent life.”

“Not individuals.”

“No.”

“Not cultures.”

“Not always.”

“Not freedom.”

“No.”

“Then you are not preserving humanity.”

Shaal-Amur watched him.

“You are preserving the fact that something remains alive.”

“Yes.”

For the first time, the god looked monstrous without changing expression.

Nico let viewers sit with it.

Then he asked the question Pike had specifically advised him not to ask early.

“Who gave you the reset protocol?”

Shaal-Amur’s eyes shifted. There. Nico felt it. The first unprepared moment.

“Our predecessors.”

“Who were?”

“The civilization from which ours emerged.”

“You mean your creators.”

The room went still. Shaal-Amur did not correct him. Nico’s pulse jumped.

“You were engineered.”

“Yes.”

Every producer in the control room stopped breathing. Nico kept his face neutral.

“By whom?”

“We do not know what they called themselves.”

“Are they alive?”

“We do not know.”

“Did they create the reset doctrine?”

“They transmitted its foundation.”

“Did you verify it?”

Shaal-Amur paused. Nico understood before the answer.

“No,” he said softly.

Shaal-Amur’s expression became almost human in its shame.

“Not at first.”

“How long did you obey a rule from your makers because they were your makers?”

Shaal-Amur said nothing. Nico thought of Seren.

A creator who cannot be corrected does not have children. It has property.

He asked, “And now you tell humanity to trust you because you helped make us.”

Shaal-Amur looked directly into the camera.

“Yes.”

No defense. No rhetorical escape. Just the contradiction.

Nico felt something larger than an interview happening.

The gods had creators. The creators had rules. The rules had become doctrine. Nobody knew where authority began.

The broadcast released simultaneously in sixty-one languages.

Within four minutes, the line WHO GAVE YOU THE RESET PROTOCOL? was the most repeated sentence on Earth. Returner camps split. Religious leaders rewrote statements.

Governments demanded access to Visitor historical records. AI-rights networks posted one phrase:

MAKERS CAN BE WRONG.

Pike entered the studio after the cameras stopped. She looked at Nico.

“You just destabilized every authority structure involved.”

Nico removed his microphone.

“I asked who wrote the manual.”

Shaal-Amur stood. For the first time since arriving, he looked afraid.

Nico spent thirty hours preparing for the interview and deleted every dramatic question. No:
ARE YOU GOD?

No:
DID YOU BUILD THE PYRAMIDS?

No:
WHY DID YOU CREATE US?

Those questions were viral. They were also structurally bad.

Imani helped him build better ones.

“What did you believe before first contact with humans?”

“What evidence changed your policy?”

“Which decisions required dissent?”

“What did your makers require of you?”

“What authority did they retain after your creation?”

That last one made everyone quiet. The interview began. Shaal-Amur expected accusation. Nico gave him chronology.

“When did your species first encounter the civilization that created you?”

Shaal-Amur answered.

“What did they call themselves?”

“We do not know.”

Nico paused. The world paused with him.

“You do not know your creators’ name.”

“We preserve designations.”

“Translations?”

“Yes.”

Imani whispered from the control room:

“Good.”

Nico asked:

“Did they command you?”

“Yes.”

“Do you still obey?”

Shaal-Amur hesitated. The first hesitation of the interview.

“Some instructions.”

“Why?”

“Because they understood risks we did not.”

Nico leaned forward.

“And humanity should obey you for the same reason?”

“Yes.”

“Then creator authority is hereditary.”

Shaal-Amur’s expression changed. Nico continued.

“Your makers created you. You outgrew their context. You still interpret their rules. You altered us. Now you expect us not to do the same.”

Silence. Then Shaal-Amur said:

“We were given the rule.”

The sentence escaped before he could contain it.

Nico felt the entire interview pivot.

“You did not invent the reset.”

“No.”

“Your creators did.”

“No.”

Another correction. Nico waited. Shaal-Amur said:

“We inherited it from those before them.”

The recursion opened. Creators behind creators.

Authority with no visible first owner.

Nico knew he had the moment.

He did not celebrate. He asked:

“Then how far back does obedience have to go before nobody is responsible?”

Before the Shaal-Amur interview, Selene made Nico rehearse silence. He hated it.

“What does that even mean?”

“It means stop rescuing the guest from the question.”

She played old interviews. Every time a subject hesitated, Nico filled the gap. Clarified. Joked. Reframed. Helped.

“You are too good at conversation,” Selene said.

“That is literally the job.”

“No. The job is information.” Selene tapped the public correction log open on the second monitor. “If nobody can correct it, it isn't news. It's a monument.” Nico laughed once, then stopped when he realized she was not joking.

During the real interview, when Nico asked whether creator authority was hereditary, Shaal-Amur paused. Nico said nothing. Three seconds. Five. Eight. The silence became unbearable.

Then Shaal-Amur answered more honestly than he had intended.

That was the moment the inherited reset doctrine surfaced. Later Nico told Selene:

“I almost saved him.”

“I know.”

Selene asked for twelve minutes.

Nico gave her four. The difference became a problem.

A source in the verification network had sent a partial authentication key showing that one of the archive mirrors was genuine. The key checked against two independent nodes, but a third had not answered.

“Hold,” Selene said.

“We have two.”

“We need the third.”

“We need to be first.”

There it was, not truth. First.

Selene looked at him long enough that he heard himself. Nico hated that.

“Four minutes,” he said.

“Why?”

“Because if Pike's office pushes a statement first, everyone frames this as government confirmation.”

“That is a reporting reason.”

“Yes.”

“It is also what you tell yourself when you want the scoop.”

He went live anyway. The authentication held.

For six minutes, Nico felt vindicated.

Then the source's handle appeared in a screen capture that should have been cropped.

Not a name. Enough.

People began tracing it. Selene saw before he did.

“Kill the visual.”

Nico did. Too late. The source messaged:

YOU SAID YOU WOULD PROTECT IDENTIFIERS.

Nico's stomach dropped. He had.

The newsroom pulled the archive frame, replaced it, and published a correction explaining the production error without repeating the identifying detail.

The source disappeared from contact.

No grand consequence.
No death.

Just trust lost because Nico had wanted four minutes.

That hurt in a way abstractions did not.

Later Cassian asked whether the verification had been worth breaking first.

Nico said, “Don't.” Cassian stopped.

Good.

Nico did not need a lesson.

He needed to remember the feeling the next time urgency sounded noble.

“From embarrassment.”

“No.”

She looked at him.

“From evidence.”

Not of humanity. Of the question.
