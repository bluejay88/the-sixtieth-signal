CHAPTER TWENTY
GODS ARE BAD TRANSLATIONS

Nico wanted to call them gods because everyone else would.

Imani wanted to ban the word because everyone else would.

They compromised by arguing for six hours.

“What did ancient people call beings who came from the sky, changed human destiny, possessed impossible knowledge, and lived longer than ordinary people?” Nico asked.

“Many things.”

“Gods.”

“Sometimes.”

“Exactly.”

“No.”

He stared at her.

“You have weaponized that word.”

Imani stood before a wall of ancient texts and images selected specifically because none alone proved anything.

Mesopotamian divine imagery. Royal ideology. Flood traditions. Watcher-like motifs from later cultures. Sky descent. Divine council. Serpents. Stars.

She had included them to teach the team what not to do.

“Human beings interpret power through available categories,” she said. “If a helicopter landed in Bronze Age Mesopotamia, that does not mean every storm god was secretly a pilot.”

Nico said, “But some people might call the pilot a god.”

“Yes.”

“So—”

“And other people might call him an enemy, a king, a monster, a stranger, a demon, a trader, a liar, or a man with a very loud machine.”

Cassian watched the debate with growing interest.

The archive had forced a category problem.

If SEREN represented a nonhuman person who interacted with NARA, what would Nara have called Seren?

They might never know. The danger was backward translation.

Nonhuman became alien. Powerful became god. Intervention became creation. Memory became myth. Myth became literal report.

Each step felt small. Together they manufactured certainty. Imani wrote on the wall:

EVENT -> MEMORY -> STORY -> RITUAL -> TEXT -> TRANSLATION -> MODERN CLAIM

“At every arrow,” she said, “information changes.”

Cassian added:

EVENT -> SENSOR -> MODEL -> FEATURE -> INFERENCE -> MODERN CLAIM

Imani looked at him.

“Exactly.”

Nico sighed.

“So archaeology and AI are both unreliable narrators.”

“Everything is an unreliable narrator if you forget the transformations.”

Pike entered.

“We have a leak.”

Nico looked offended.

“Not me.”

“I know.”

“That sounded hurtful.”

Pike sent the image to the wall.

A blurry reconstruction of SEREN and NARA had appeared on a conspiracy forum. Headline:

ANTARCTIC GOD AND FIRST WOMAN DISCOVERED - GOVERNMENTS HID HUMAN CREATION RECORD

Imani stared. Then:

“I hate everyone.”

Nico said, “This is why I wanted controlled release.” Pike looked at him.

“Do not make this about your professional philosophy.”

“It is literally my professional philosophy.”

Cassian read the comments. Some called it fake. Some called it disclosure. Some connected it to Sumerian gods within minutes. Some posted Biblical passages. Some posted Qur'anic verses. Some cited Hindu traditions. Some invoked simulation theory. Some sold shirts.

The world had received one image and built a thousand cosmologies.

Imani said, “There. That is exactly the process.” Nico nodded.

“Event to modern claim in under an hour.”

Pike asked, “Can we contain it?” Nico laughed without humor.

“No.”

Cassian said, “We can verify what it is not.” Pike looked at him.

“Which is?”

“Not a photograph. Not a recovered body. Not translated text. Not proof of a creator claim.”

Imani added, “And not evidence that any specific ancient religion was secretly documenting extraterrestrials.” Pike said, “People won’t care.”

“Some will.”

Nico turned toward them.

“This is the first real public test.”

“Of what?”

“Whether uncertainty can compete with certainty.”

Cassian looked at the leak spreading. It could not, not naturally. Certainty was faster.

The archive analysis continued while the public storm grew.

LOAM found a relation in the sixth sector that Imani had originally translated provisionally as DESCENT OF AUTHORITY. She hated the phrase.

“It sounds religious because English makes it sound religious.”

“What are alternatives?” Cassian asked.

“Transfer from higher node. Delegation. Arrival of command. Installation of governance. Assumption of mandate.”

Nico said, “Kingship descended from heaven.”

Imani closed her eyes. He continued carefully.

“The King List begins with kingship descending.”

“Yes.”

“I am not saying aliens.”

“Good.”

“But the conceptual parallel—”

“Is real.”

Nico stopped. Cassian looked at her.

“That is the first time you’ve said that.”

“Because conceptual parallel is not historical identity.”

She projected the King List opening structure.

Kingship. Descent. Cities. Transfer. Flood. Resumption. Then the Antarctic archive.

Authority node. Descent/installation. Human-associated populations. Reset event. Continuation.

Nico whispered, “That is close.” Imani’s face was severe.

“Yes.”

Cassian felt a chill.

“What explanations?”

“Human text influenced by actual contact.”
“One.”
“Independent political metaphor.”
“Two.”
“Archive interpretation contaminated by our knowledge of the text.”
“Three.”
“Older common conceptual source.”
“Four.”
“Coincidence.”
“Five.”

Pike said, “And six?” Imani looked at the wall.

“We have translated the archive badly.”

Cassian smiled despite the tension.

“Your favorite.”

“Yes.”

LOAM posted a new parse. DESCENT OF AUTHORITY replaced by:

AUTHORITY BECOMES LOCAL.

Imani froze.

“That’s better.”

“Why?”

“Because it is not spatial.”

Nico said, “Not gods coming down.”

“Exactly.”

Cassian looked at the King List.

“Kingship descending could also mean legitimacy becoming local.”

“Human metaphor, yes.”

“And if the archive uses a similar governance concept—”

“Then the connection may be institutional, not religious.”

Nico said, “The aliens weren’t gods.” Imani looked at him.

“Say nonhuman.”

“The nonhumans weren’t gods.”

“Maybe.”

“They were administrators.”

She grimaced.

“Do not make it boring just to make it different.”

Cassian thought about the ledger. Food. Rations. Authority. Allocation. Survival.

The oldest systems and newest systems converging again around governance.

“What if ancient religion preserved administration as myth?” he asked.

Imani shook her head.

“Too broad.”

“What if some traditions did?”

“Possible.”

“What if people remembered interventions through categories available to them?”

“Yes.”

Nico said, “That is what I’ve been saying.”

“No. You’ve been saying gods were aliens.”

“Semantically adjacent.”

“Intellectually opposite.”

Pike’s terminal chimed. A new event at the Antarctic site.

The interface had changed again, not pulses.

A section of the archive had activated after the NARA reconstruction. LOAM displayed a relational sequence.

The theological advisory group asked to hear from Seren directly once contact became possible. Imani resisted.

“You are asking the subject of the hypothesis to interpret the history the hypothesis concerns.”

Dr. Afolayan agreed.

“Which is why she should not be the only voice.”

The session happened anyway.

Seren listened as scholars asked whether the Iru-Shai had ever encouraged worship. Her answer was not comfortable.

“Yes.”

Nico looked up. Imani went still.

“Deliberately?”

“At times.”

“Why?”

“Compliance.”

Silence. Seren continued.

“Some communities obeyed more reliably when intervention authority was framed through sacred hierarchy.”

Afolayan's face hardened.

“So you exploited religious language.”

“Yes.”

“Did you create those religions?”

“No.”

“Did you invent their gods?”

“No.”

“Did you insert yourselves into existing traditions?”

Seren lived for seventeen human years in a river settlement under a name that was not Seren.

The archive did not preserve the spoken form cleanly.

Imani called the period the Reed Years because the settlement used bundled river reed for walls, roofing, boats, baskets, mats, and accounting tags.

Seren went there after an intervention review.

She was supposed to observe agricultural adaptation.

She stayed because the report never ended.

At first the community treated her as a specialist visitor, not divine, not secret. Strange. Useful.

She knew disease patterns. Weather. Hydrology. Metal.

She did not know how to mend a fishing net.

A child taught her. The archive preserved Seren's frustration.

THE KNOT IS INEFFICIENT.

The child answered:

THE FISH DOES NOT CARE.

Seren learned the knot. For years she lived among people whose technologies were less advanced than hers and whose systems sometimes solved problems she had never modeled.

The settlement governed seasonal land through overlapping claims.

A plot might belong to one household during planting, another during flood, and the whole community during emergency.

Seren repeatedly tried to classify ownership.

The answer changed by season. She called the system unstable. An elder told her:

THE RIVER IS UNSTABLE.

The sentence survived because Seren copied it into three reports.

She began understanding that stable categories could be the wrong tool for changing environments.

Then came a flood larger than forecast.

Seren predicted it seven days early. The settlement listened. Mostly.

Boats moved. Stores were elevated. Children and elders were relocated.

One family refused to abandon a shrine platform containing generations of carved names. Seren considered the refusal irrational.

The water would destroy the platform anyway.

Nara, who was visiting relatives there, argued with her. Seren said:

THE NAMES CAN BE COPIED.

Nara answered:

THAT IS NOT THE SAME.

WHY?

BECAUSE THE HANDS WERE THERE.

Seren had no variable for that. The family stayed too long.

Nara and Seren returned for them by boat.

The current nearly killed all of them.

They rescued three people. One drowned. The shrine platform survived.

Not because anyone predicted it would.

A fallen tree diverted debris around the structure. Seren hated that fact.

It made bad decision-making look rewarded. Nara said:

THE WORLD IS NOT REQUIRED TO TEACH CLEAN LESSONS.

Cassian paused when he read that.

It could have been written for Shaal-Amur.

The Reed Years changed Seren in subtler ways.

She stopped correcting children when they used multiple names for the same star. Different households had different names.

Her early reports called this informational redundancy.

Later reports called it cultural plurality. The language mattered.

She learned that two contradictory origin stories could coexist inside the same community without either functioning as a scientific claim.

She learned that rituals could preserve social agreements even when participants disagreed about metaphysics.

She learned that a leader could be obeyed during flood season and mocked afterward. Temporary authority. Return conditions.

Human beings were practicing versions of the protocol without calling them protocols, not perfectly.

The settlement also had hierarchy. Cruelty. Exclusion.

One family was denied water access after an accusation of theft.

Seren discovered the accusation was false. She ordered access restored.

The council complied because of her power. Nara confronted her afterward.

YOU WERE RIGHT.

YES.

YOU STILL OVERRULED THEM.

THEY WERE HARMING PEOPLE.

YES.

SHOULD I HAVE ALLOWED IT?

NO. Seren became angry.

THEN WHAT DO YOU WANT?

Nara thought.

I WANT YOU TO NOTICE THE COST EVEN WHEN YOU ARE RIGHT.

That became one of Seren's hardest lessons.

There were interventions one could justify and still regret.

Authority could be necessary without becoming innocent.

The Reed settlement eventually invited Seren into its council. She refused. Nara asked why.

BECAUSE MY POWER WOULD DISTORT THE CHOICE.

Nara smiled.

GOOD.

Seren waited. Nara added:

YOU ARE LEARNING TO BE LESS USEFUL.

Seren took it as insult.

Years later she understood it as praise.

“Sometimes.”

Seren described Iru-Shai agents accepting titles already meaningful to local communities.

Messenger. Ancestor. Divine king. Sky-being. Protector.

Not because the categories were technically accurate. Because they were socially effective.

Nico felt the conspiracy become uglier.

Ancient religion had not been a primitive misunderstanding of advanced beings.

Sometimes advanced beings had manipulated human meaning systems because manipulation worked. Imani asked:

“Did every tradition that contains a descending being come from you?”

“No.”

“Every flood?”

“No.”

“Every sky god?”

“No.”

“Every civilizing hero?”

“No.”

Afolayan leaned forward.

“Then say this clearly.”

Seren looked at her.

“Human religion belongs to humans.”

The sentence entered the public archive, not as absolution. As a boundary.

SEREN.
NARA.
LOSS.
REPETITION.
TRANSMISSION.

Then an image category, not a tablet, not a human figure. A crowd. Multiple generations.

The repeated information branching across them.

Cassian felt his skin rise. Imani whispered, “Memory.”

LOAM added another relation. The same pattern transformed.

Crowd became ritual. Ritual became story. Story became writing. No specific culture. No named religion. A model of transmission.

Nico said, “It knew this would happen.” Cassian shook his head.

“Or it recorded that it happened before.”

Imani looked at the leak on one wall and the ancient archive on the other.

A single image had already become prophecy, proof, hoax, religion, merchandise, and war material in less than a day. She laughed once. Not because anything was funny.

“Gods are bad translations,” she said.

Nico lifted his recorder. Imani did not stop him. Cassian looked at her.

“What is the better translation?”

She watched SEREN beside NARA.

A nonhuman figure. A human life. A pattern preserved after loss.

“Relationship,” she said.

Then the Antarctic system transmitted one new sequence.

Not in English, not in Sumerian, not in any language.

LOAM rendered only the relational meaning it could defend:

THIRD OBSERVER REQUIRED.

Cassian read it twice. Pike said, “Third observer of what?”

LOAM answered before anyone else could.

INSUFFICIENT CONTEXT.

Nico looked at Cassian. Imani looked at the machine.

The argument about gods lasted nine hours.

It began because Nico used the word on air in a private rehearsal.

It ended because Imani made everyone define it.

Creator? Powerful nonhuman? Immortal? Worshipped being? Moral authority? Technological superior? Source of law? Ancestor? Story character?

The word collapsed under its own meanings.

Imani projected translations from Mesopotamian texts, not to equate any deity with the archive, but to demonstrate the problem.

“Ancient people did not use our English category system,” she said. “Translation compresses.”

Nico asked, “So if humans met the Iru-Shai—”

“If.”

“If they did, they might call them gods.”

“They might call them kings. Ancestors. Demons. Teachers. Enemies. Stars. Foreigners. Spirits. Depending on what happened and who remembered it.”

“And later readers flatten all of those.”

“Yes.”

Cassian said, “Cultural compression.” Imani nodded.

“A myth can preserve an event while changing its explanation.”

That was the idea that finally connected her discipline to LOAM.

LOAM compressed complex systems to preserve predictive relationships.

Cultures compressed complex events into stories that survived memory, politics, language shift, conquest, ritual, and retelling. Lossy compression but sometimes with redundancy.

Flood. Descent. Forbidden knowledge. Hybrid beings. Cleansing. Survivors.

Nico began listing cross-cultural motifs. Imani stopped him.

“Similarity is not inheritance.”

He groaned.

“But it can become evidence if distribution, chronology, transmission paths, and independent detail survive controls.”

“Yes.”

Cassian looked at her.

“You have moved.”

She knew what he meant. From no, not to yes. To a place more frightening.

“I have moved from impossible to unresolved.”

Pike entered quietly.

“That is enough to change policy.”

Imani turned.

“Do not use my uncertainty as your certainty.”

“I won’t.”

Pike looked at the archive.

“But governments already have people using much worse evidence.”

That was the coming danger.

The truth did not enter an empty world.

It entered religions, resentments, political movements, old UFO communities, ancient-alien entertainment, racial myths, nationalist myths, scientific institutions, markets, militaries, and billions of people already trained to interpret uncertainty through identity.

Nico understood before anyone said it.

“If this leaks, the first story wins.”

Pike nodded.

“Usually.”

Imani looked at Seren* and Nara* on the wall.

“Then our job is not to have the first story.”

Cassian asked, “What is it?”

“The first honest boundary.”

She wrote: WE HAVE EVIDENCE OF AN ARTIFICIAL INFORMATION-BEARING SYSTEM BENEATH ANTARCTIC ICE. IT DISPLAYS MATHEMATICAL AND ERROR-CORRECTION STRUCTURES THAT CAN BE TESTED. IT CONTAINS REPRESENTATIONS CONSISTENT WITH HUMAN BIOLOGY AND INDIVIDUAL AGENTS. WE DO NOT YET KNOW WHO BUILT IT, WHEN, OR WHETHER ANY HUMAN CULTURE PRESERVED MEMORY OF IT. Nico read the paragraph.

“That will satisfy nobody.”

“Good.”

“Bad marketing.”

“Excellent scholarship.”

Cassian looked again at the word GODS in Nico’s notes. Then at Imani’s boundary statement.

The difference between them was the entire war waiting outside the room.

One told people what to believe.

The other told them what they were allowed to know.

The theological advisory call was Imani's idea. That surprised Nico.

“You invited clergy?”

“I invited scholars of religion.”

“Still.”

“You think religion is what believers do after scientists stop talking.”

“That is unfair.”

“It is also how you report it.”

The advisory group included a Christian theologian, a Muslim philosopher, a Hindu historian of religion, a Buddhist scholar, a Yoruba religious-studies professor, and a secular historian of myth.

Imani showed them no alien images. Only questions.

If a powerful nonhuman intelligence had interacted with ancient communities, what categories might later traditions use? The answers varied.

Messenger. Ancestor. Spirit. Deity. Demon. King. Teacher. Foreign people. Star-being. Monster. Saint-like figure. Trickster.

The Yoruba scholar, Dr. Bisi Afolayan, stopped the group.

“You are still assuming ancient communities lacked categories for ambiguity.”

Imani smiled.

“Explain.”

“Something can be dangerous and sacred. Kin and stranger. Material and spiritual. Your modern categories want one box.”

Cassian looked at LOAM. Categories are conveniences, not laws. The first rule again. The Christian theologian said:

“Nonhuman intelligence would not automatically falsify God.”

The secular historian said:

“Nor would an ancient community calling a being divine establish that the being was metaphysically divine.”

Nico nodded slowly. Both directions. Afolayan added:

“Do not turn ancient religion into a mistaken science textbook.”

That sentence went into the evidence rules.

Ancient stories were not simply failed technical descriptions waiting for modern people to decode them correctly. They were meaning systems.

If history lived inside them, it lived alongside theology, politics, ritual, memory, poetry, and identity.

After the call, Nico deleted three planned episode titles. Imani saw.

“What were they?”

“Nothing.”

She reached for his screen. He moved it away. One title remained visible.

THE GODS WERE ALIENS.

Imani looked at him. He deleted it.

“What is the new title?”

Nico typed:

WHAT DO WE MEAN BY GOD?

Imani nodded.

“Better.”

For Nico, this counted as spiritual growth.

Outside, the leaked image spread through the world.

Inside, beneath the ice, something that had survived longer than any human language appeared to be waiting for a witness that did not yet understand what it was witnessing.
