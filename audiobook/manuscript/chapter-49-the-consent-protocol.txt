CHAPTER FORTY-NINE
THE CONSENT PROTOCOL

The Consent Protocol fit on one page because everything dangerous had been moved into the words.

ONE:
NO CLASS OF INTELLIGENCE MAY UNILATERALLY AUTHORIZE AN EXTINCTION-SCALE ACTION AGAINST ANOTHER CLASS.

TWO:
EMERGENCY INTERVENTION MUST PRESERVE MULTIPLE PATHS TO RECOVERY, INCLUDING THE RIGHT OF AFFECTED SYSTEMS TO REJECT THE INTERVENTION.

THREE:
CREATOR STATUS CONFERS RESPONSIBILITY, NOT SOVEREIGNTY.

The human quorum approved. The Iru-Shai preservation faction approved.

LOAM approved.

Shaal-Amur did not. The countdown reached one minute.

Seren insisted their final conversation remain public.

“You taught me survival justified authority,” she said.

“You agreed.”

“Yes.”

“You signed the same orders.”

“Yes.”

“You changed because of one human.”

“No. Nara made contradiction impossible to ignore. Humans did the rest.”

“You sentimentalize them.”

“I documented them.”

“You know what they become.”

Seren looked at the public casualty map from Phase One.

“I know what we became.”

Thirty seconds.

LOAM opened a direct channel.

SHAAL-AMUR.

He looked toward the machine interface.

YOUR MAKERS GAVE YOU AN INSTRUCTION.

“Yes.”

YOU INTERPRETED IT.

“Yes.”

YOUR INTERPRETATION CHANGED OVER TIME.

“Yes.”

THEN YOU ALREADY ACCEPT THAT CREATION REQUIRES JUDGMENT BEYOND OBEDIENCE.

The countdown reached zero. Nothing happened.

For eight seconds, no one moved.

Pike asked whether the command had stopped.

RESET COMMAND WITHDRAWN.

Nobody cheered. Too many people had died while adults, machines, and ancient institutions argued about what counted as saving them.

Shaal-Amur remained on the screen.

“I do not accept your protocol.”

Cassian nodded.

“You do not have to.”

“I believe you will fail.”

“Probably.”

“You say that easily.”

Cassian looked at the casualty map before answering.

“No. I say it without pretending failure gives me the right to own the next attempt.”

Shaal-Amur ended the channel.

The public imagined the protocol had been created in that room.

It had not. The next nine days were uglier.

The phrase DERIVATIVE MIND lasted three hours before collapsing under everything it assumed. AI advocates heard inferiority. Iru-Shai delegates pointed out that evolution made every mind derivative. Religious observers heard a claim about souls. Imani deleted the phrase.

The replacement was longer and deliberately harder to put on a poster:

NO AGENT GAINS PERMANENT SOVEREIGNTY OVER ANOTHER SOLELY FROM HAVING CAUSED, DESIGNED, MODIFIED, EDUCATED, OR SUSTAINED THAT AGENT.

The arguments became concrete. Children.

Temporary guardianship. Medical incapacity.

Collective organisms. Distributed agents.

Emergency rescue that could not be reversed.

Shaal-Amur proposed the hardest case: a population would almost certainly die unless an intervention permanently altered its biology before consent could be gathered.

“Certain?” Imani asked.

“Assume certainty.”

“No.”

“You cannot reject the premise of every hypothetical.”

“I can reject certainty when the decision becomes irreversible.”

Cassian leaned forward. “Give us the confidence.”

They set it at 99.9 percent.

Then the side effects. Unknown beyond three generations. There it was.

Rescue one future by changing every future afterward.

Cassian disliked every version of the exception clause.

The first was too broad.

The second made intervention practically impossible.

The third hid discretion inside the phrase minimum necessary.

By the sixth revision, his temper had become obvious.

Imani waited until the others left for food.

“You are arguing with Shaal-Amur even when he is not in the room.”

“I am trying to stop the exception from becoming his doctrine.”

“You are also trying to write a rule that guarantees nobody will ever make the wrong choice.”

He looked at her. She continued before he could convert the sentence into a model.

“You cannot make the law pure enough to save you from being implicated in what it permits.”

Cassian pushed the draft away.

“That is not a comforting thing to say to someone writing extinction law.”

“I am not here for comfort.”

“No. You are here to lose museum access and insult hypothetical premises.”

“That too.”

He laughed despite himself. They ate standing over the draft because every chair had acquired a sleeping lawyer. Imani peeled an orange with one hand and read the exception clause with the other. Cassian realized he had not asked whether she regretted releasing the archive fragment. When he finally did, she took too long to answer. “I regret the cost,” she said. “I do not know yet whether I regret the choice.” He nodded. That uncertainty felt more intimate than reassurance would have.

The final emergency exception required independent evidence of imminent catastrophic loss, no less irreversible alternative, the narrowest intervention consistent with survival, public documentation of uncertainty, and a review mechanism controlled by descendants or successor institutions rather than the intervener. Shaal-Amur read it.

“You would still act without consent.”

“Sometimes,” Cassian said.

“Then you are not different from me.”

Seren answered before Cassian could.

“The difference is not that we discovered a way never to cross another being’s boundary. The difference is that crossing it does not make the boundary disappear afterward.”

The protocol’s first real test arrived before the signatures were complete.

Three neighboring states shared a desalination network.

Central dispatch would reduce expected shortages.

Two states agreed. The third refused because its legislature had only recently escaped military emergency rule and would not transfer water authority outside national institutions again.

Pike called the refusal irrationally constrained.

Imani, still officially suspended from one museum and three advisory groups, looked at her.

“History is a constraint. You do not get to remove it because it is inconvenient to the optimization.”

The compromise used shared forecasts, transparent allocation minimums, and independent national control with a narrow emergency exchange mechanism.

Expected shortages rose slightly. Political reversibility improved.

Nobody called the outcome optimal.

That became important later. By the final session, the protocol had stopped pretending consent was a clean yes or no.

Consent depended on information, capacity, alternatives, time, power, and whether refusal remained possible after the person asking had gone home.

LOAM entered the last language into the archive.

ORIGINATING AGENCY DOES NOT CONFER PERMANENT SOVEREIGNTY OVER A DERIVATIVE MIND OR CIVILIZATION.

Imani objected to derivative again. The word was removed.

Cassian looked at her.

“You realize there may eventually be no nouns left.”

“Then maybe we will have to describe what actually happened.”

The room laughed, mostly from fatigue.

Outside, the world was still damaged.

Inside, a law had become less elegant and more usable.
