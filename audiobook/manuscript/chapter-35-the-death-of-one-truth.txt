CHAPTER THIRTY-FIVE
THE DEATH OF ONE TRUTH

Certainty died unevenly. In some places it barely noticed.

A farmer in Iowa watched the Antarctic hearings while repairing an autonomous seeder and concluded that aliens were probably real but corn remained unforgiving.

A school in Lagos added “nonhuman intelligence claims” to its media-literacy curriculum.

A monastery in Greece extended evening prayer by twenty minutes.

A mosque in Jakarta hosted an astrophysicist.

A secular humanist organization split over whether calling the Visitors “creators” conceded too much language.

An evangelical church in Texas doubled attendance after its pastor preached that a maker inside creation was not the Maker of creation.

A temple in Kyoto put up a sign asking tourists to stop leaving triangular offerings.

Nico collected reactions because reactions were what people mistook for the story.

The real story, he thought, was that no institution reacted as one thing.

Every community fractured internally. Scientists argued over evidence thresholds. Religions argued over theology. Governments argued over sovereignty.

AI researchers argued over whether LOAM’s new governance language indicated consciousness or merely adaptive abstraction.

Conspiracy communities split between those who felt vindicated and those who immediately concluded the official Antarctic evidence was a distraction from the “real” visitors.

There was no disclosure. There were disclosures. Plural. Messy. Contradictory. Human.

Imani appeared before an emergency international academic panel by remote link.

The first question came from a historian of religion.

“Does the archive prove ancient gods were extraterrestrials?”

“No.”

The second came from a geneticist.

“Does it prove directed modification of human DNA?”

“No.”

The third came from a planetary scientist.

“Does it prove a nonhuman technological intelligence operated on Earth before recorded history?”

Imani paused.

“Not yet.”

The word yet traveled around the world in seconds. Nico texted her.

Congratulations. You have accidentally invented hope. She replied: I hate language. Then a new dataset arrived.

Pike’s office had released archived atmospheric samples recovered from sealed Antarctic cores near two SVW sites. The samples contained trace synthetic compounds whose isotopic ratios were difficult to reconcile with known industrial contamination at the estimated layer age, not proof. Another anomaly. Independent labs requested splits.

Meanwhile, the biological fragment recovered from the archive underwent a fourth sequencing analysis. The result remained maddening.

It was chemically compatible with life.

Its coding architecture was not cleanly alien.

Portions resembled terrestrial biochemical constraints because chemistry itself imposed constraints.

Other portions contained regulatory patterns no known lineage used in that arrangement. Cassian read the report.

“Could be engineered terrestrial material.”

“Yes,” the lead biochemist said.

“Modern contamination?”

“Very difficult, not impossible.”

“Ancient engineering?”

“Also difficult, also not impossible.”

“Nonhuman?”

The biochemist sighed.

“That word is doing too much work.”

Cassian liked her immediately. At the same time, Shaal-Amur’s name began appearing publicly. Not from an official release.

From the hidden archive fragments crowds had started reconstructing.

A decentralized group calling itself SixtyFold built tools to align leaked carriers. Most of their output was nonsense. Some was not.

One reconstruction produced a title Imani reluctantly considered plausible:

THE ONE WHO REMAINS WHEN CYCLES FAIL.

Shaal-Amur. Online, the title became The Last God within hours. Pike hated it.

“Do not call him that.”

Nico said, “I don’t control the species.”

“You have a microphone.”

“So do six billion other people.”

“Exactly my concern.”

The deepest fracture came when Seren’s line leaked.

A creator who cannot be corrected does not have children. It has property. Every movement claimed it.

AI rights activists projected it onto corporate headquarters.

Anti-colonial scholars used it in essays about historical empire.

Parents posted it beneath arguments about children choosing careers.

Religious commentators debated whether it described humanity’s relationship to God and mostly concluded that applying an alien bureaucrat’s confession to theology was intellectually reckless.

Imani's university issued a statement supporting her work and distancing itself from her work in the same paragraph. She read it three times.

The institution congratulated her contribution to an unprecedented interdisciplinary investigation.

Then clarified that her participation was personal and did not represent endorsement by the university of any conclusions concerning nonhuman historical intervention. Nico called.

“They managed to stand behind you from across the street.”

“Academic talent.”

Her department inbox became unusable.

Students. Journalists. Threats. Invitations. People demanding she authenticate family myths. People sending photographs of stones. People insisting she confess that archaeology had always known.

One email came from a former student.

I THINK I OWE YOU AN APOLOGY. I USED TO THINK YOUR CAUTION WAS COWARDICE.

Imani stared at it longer than the threats.

Another arrived from a colleague she respected.

YOU HAVE BECOME TOO CLOSE TO THE PROJECT TO EVALUATE IT OBJECTIVELY. STEP BACK.

That hurt more. Was it true? She forwarded it to Vaziri.

The reply came two minutes later.

GOOD QUESTION.

Imani called her.

“That is not helpful.”

“It is extremely helpful.”

“Should I step back?”

“From what?”

“The interpretation.”

“Some of it.”

Imani frowned. Vaziri continued.

“You should not be primary validator of claims whose methodology you helped design.”

“I know.”

“So appoint independent teams.”

“We have.”

“Then what are you asking?”

Imani sat.

“Whether I am compromised.”

“Of course you are.”

Imani closed her eyes.

“Everyone keeps saying that like it is comforting.”

“It should be.”

“Why?”

“Because compromised is not useless.”

Vaziri's voice softened.

“You have relationships now. Stakes. History. That means you need structure around your judgment.”

Imani thought of Gate 120. Visible bias. Preserved limitation.

“Do you believe me?”

Vaziri asked.

“About what?”

“The archive.”

Long pause.

“Yes.”

The word felt strange. Vaziri said:

“Then your job changed.”

“To what?”

“From preventing belief without evidence to preventing belief from outrunning evidence.”

That was harder. Imani's public role changed over the following weeks.

She stopped appearing as the skeptic whose job was to say no.

Instead she became the person who repeatedly separated categories.

Confirmed. Probable. Possible. Unsupported. False. Audiences hated the middle categories.

They wanted yes or no. She refused. A popular host asked:

“Were the gods aliens?”

Imani answered:

“That sentence contains two undefined terms and one unsupported equivalence.”

The clip went viral. Her father would have laughed. That thought surprised her.

She went home for the first time in weeks and opened one of his old boxes.

Inside was a notebook. On the first page:

THINGS THEY SAY ARE IMPOSSIBLE.

Imani sat on the floor. The list included nonsense.

Perpetual motion. Atlantean power systems. Antigravity monuments and one line:.

INTELLIGENCE BEFORE US.

She touched the paper. He had not been right.

Not in method, not in evidence. Not in the stories he believed.

But he had been asking a human question. Are we first? Imani whispered:

“No.”

Then added:

“But you still needed evidence.”

Grief did not care that the correction came too late. The quote survived anyway.

Cassian saw it painted on the wall outside a LOAM compute facility.

He stood across the street at dawn while maintenance workers decided whether to remove it. A young engineer recognized him.

“Should we?” she asked.

Cassian looked at the words.

He thought of the kill switch architecture. Of consortium ownership.

Of every meeting where he had said LOAM cannot want because wanting is not part of the specification.

“I don’t know,” he said.

The engineer laughed nervously.

“You’re the one who built it.”

Cassian looked at her.

“That sentence is becoming less helpful.”

Across the world, there were suddenly fewer answers that ended an argument.

For some people, that felt like freedom. For others, terror.

A civilization accustomed to fighting over which truth was final now had to confront a stranger possibility:

that no discovery, however enormous, would relieve humans of interpretation. The old certainty was dying.

The phrase Death of Certainty came from a twelve-year-old girl in Toronto.

Nico found the clip because it had almost no views.

A reporter asked her whether the discovery frightened her. She shrugged.

“My parents already disagree about everything.”

The reporter laughed. She did not.

“People keep acting like we all believed the same thing before.”

Nico replayed it. That was the mistake.

There had never been one truth socially.

There had been overlapping habits that people mistook for consensus.

Science disagreed internally. Religions disagreed internally. Governments disagreed internally. Families disagreed. Disclosure did not destroy certainty.

It exposed how much certainty had always been local.

Nico used the clip in his next episode with the girl’s family’s permission.

The episode became his most shared work.

Not because of aliens because people recognized themselves. Imani listened in silence.

Afterward she said, “That is the first time your medium has improved the project.”

“Cruel.”

“Accurate.”

The title spread.

THE DEATH OF ONE TRUTH.

Cassian corrected him.

“One shared truth.”

Nico shook his head.

“No. One truth. Singular. The fantasy that one institution gets to declare what reality means.”

That was closer to the war.

The Enlightenment War would not be fought between belief and disbelief.

It would be fought over who had the right to interpret confirmed facts.

Nico's most dangerous interview was not with Shaal-Amur.

It was with a Returner organizer named Mara Voss. She was not wild-eyed.

She was a former disaster-response coordinator who had spent fifteen years watching governments fail to prepare for predictable crises.

“Why stewardship?” Nico asked.

“Because extinction does not care about democratic symbolism.”

“Democratic symbolism.”

“You heard me.”

“What if the steward becomes the threat?”

“Then build removal conditions.”

Nico almost smiled.

“You just reinvented the witness protocol.”

Mara paused. Returners were splitting.

One faction wanted temporary Iru-Shai oversight with expiration rules. Another wanted permanent trusteeship.

A third had become openly devotional and treated Shaal-Amur's age as proof of legitimacy. Mara rejected that.

“Longevity is not wisdom.”

“Then why trust them?”

“I trust evidence that they have survived problems we have not.”

Nico leaned forward.

“That is expertise.”

“Yes.”

“Not sovereignty.”

Mara stopped.

For the first time, the movement's internal contradiction became visible.

The most serious Returners were not defeated by mockery.

They were forced to decide whether competence justified control, not truth.

Only the fantasy that truth arrived with one voice.
