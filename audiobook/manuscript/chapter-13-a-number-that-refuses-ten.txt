CHAPTER THIRTEEN
A NUMBER THAT REFUSES TEN

Cassian trusted numbers most when they refused to be impressive.

The Antarctic sequence looked terrible in base ten. That pleased him.

It produced awkward fractions, irregular carries, ugly residuals.

In base twelve it improved. In base twenty it improved differently. In base sixty it became boring. Boring was dangerous.

Boring meant the representation might fit the thing rather than force it.

Imani stood beside him while LOAM displayed the same sequence under four number systems.

Nico had named the visualization THE FOUR GOSPELS.

Imani had threatened to throw him out.

“Why sixty?” Cassian asked.

“Because sixty is divisible.”

“That’s the human explanation.”

“Yes.”

“What’s the system explanation?”

Imani looked at him.

“You’re asking whether a nonhuman engineer would independently choose it.”

“Yes.”

“Maybe. If the problem rewards many clean subdivisions.”

“Angles.”

“Time.”

“Synchronization.”

“Allocation.”

“Fractions.”

Cassian thought of food. A warehouse divided among two regions. Three. Four. Five. Six.

A base that made common sharing easy.

LOAM had originally used binary underneath everything, of course. Machines did not need decimal sentiment. But human-facing optimization frequently represented quantities in whichever units minimized operational error.

“Could sixty be a governance number?” Cassian asked.

Imani laughed once.

“That is a very strange sentence.”

“Think about it. If you need systems divided among many coalition sizes—”

“Two, three, four, five, six.”

“Exactly.”

“You’re turning arithmetic into politics.”

“Humans did first.”

She considered.

“Maybe.”

Nico entered carrying three coffees and a printed image.

“Speaking of humans ruining numbers.”

Imani took the coffee but not the bait.

“What is that?”

“King List.”

She closed her eyes.

“No.”

“You haven’t—”

“No.”

Cassian smiled. Nico placed the image down anyway.

The Weld-Blundell Prism, four-sided, densely inscribed.

“I know what it is,” Imani said.

“I know you know.”

“Then why are you showing me?”

“Because LOAM flagged the reign lengths.”

Imani turned to Cassian.

“You let it search the King List?”

“Not for alien matches.”

“Then for what?”

“Normalization tests.”

She set down the coffee.

“Explain carefully.”

Cassian did. They had asked LOAM to search selected ancient corpora for mathematical structures that remained detectable after language was removed.

Not similarity in gods, not floods, not sky beings, not creation stories. Numerical residue.

The Sumerian King List was an obvious stress test because its earliest reign lengths were famously enormous and often treated as mythic.

LOAM normalized the reign values in several ways. Most produced nothing useful.

One produced repeated residue classes under sexagesimal decomposition. Imani’s expression hardened.

“Which version?”

Cassian named it.

“Variant differences?”

“Not yet fully integrated.”

“Then it means nothing.”

“I know.”

“Copying errors?”

“In progress.”

“Modern editorial reconstruction?”

“In progress.”

“Selection bias?”

“Controlled by applying the same method to other royal lists.”

She relaxed slightly. Nico said, “Do you interrogate everyone like this?”

“Yes.”

“I’m starting to understand academia.”

Imani pulled the King List data closer.

“Before anyone gets excited: this is not a technical document.”

Cassian nodded.

“It is ideological. It organizes kingship. It puts cities and rulers into a sequence, includes impossible reign lengths, and crosses a Flood boundary. Different copies differ. It is not a pristine timestamp from prehistory.”

Nico said, “But the impossible ages—”

“Are not proof of aliens.”

“I know.”

“Say it.”

“The impossible ages are not proof of aliens.”

“Good.”

Cassian watched the numbers.

“What if they are not ages?”

Imani did not answer immediately.

“That question has been asked many times.”

“I don’t mean secretly encoded spacecraft cycles.”

“Thank you.”

“What if a later scribe copied values whose original unit had changed?”

“That is plausible in principle.”

“Or a mathematical relationship survived after semantic meaning failed.”

“That is also plausible in principle.”

Nico looked between them.

“You two are dangerously close to agreeing.”

Imani ignored him.

“Show me the residue.”

LOAM decomposed the pre-Flood reign values.

The exact numbers varied by ruler and copy tradition, but repeated divisibility patterns emerged.

Too many zeros. Too many large round values. Sexagesimal friendliness. Historically unsurprising.

Ancient Mesopotamian numbers often reflected sexagesimal conventions. Imani said so. Cassian nodded.

Then LOAM applied a transformation derived entirely from the Antarctic checksum—not invented from the King List.

The residue pattern tightened. Imani stopped.

“What transformation?”

Cassian showed her. No words. No mythology. No hand-picked ruler names.

A six-stage remainder operation based on the Antarctic damage-recovery model.

The King List produced the same broad residue family as the Antarctic marks.

Not exact, not miraculous. Similar enough to test.

Imani walked away from the table.

Nico whispered, “Is that good?” Cassian said, “It’s interesting.”

“That is scientist for yes.”

“No. It is scientist for keep going.”

Imani returned with a notebook.

“We need controls.”

“Already running.”

“Later Mesopotamian king lists.”

“Yes.”

“Egyptian regnal records.”

“Yes.”

“Chinese dynastic sequences.”

“Yes.”

“Randomized values preserving distribution.”

“Yes.”

“Modern sports statistics.”

Nico laughed. Cassian said, “Why?”

“Because if your transformation can find cosmic truth in basketball seasons, I want to know now.”

Cassian added the control. LOAM processed.

The Antarctic transform produced weak matches in many datasets. Expected.

It produced stronger matches in sexagesimal-heavy Mesopotamian material. Also expected.

The King List sat above most controls, not alone, not decisive. Imani stared.

“Could be number culture.”

Cassian nodded.

“Humans using the same mathematical habits in multiple contexts.”

“Yes.”

“Which would still be interesting.”

“Yes.”

Nico said, “Because?” Imani answered.

“Because if the Antarctic structure is genuinely independent, and if it shares the same arithmetic habits, we need to explain direction.”

Nico’s eyes lit.

“Visitors taught humans.”

“No.”

He groaned.

“Alternative one: coincidence.”
She raised a finger.
“Alternative two: similar problems produce similar mathematics.”
Second finger.
“Alternative three: humans influenced whatever is under the ice.”
Third.
“Alternative four: some older common source influenced both.”
Fourth.
“Alternative five: our analysis pipeline is manufacturing convergence.”

Cassian said, “That’s why we need an independent discriminator.” LOAM posted:

CHECKSUM CANDIDATE AVAILABLE.

Vaziri invited Imani to present the Antarctic numerical work to a closed seminar under one condition.

“No images.”

Nico objected.

“Why?”

“Because images seduce.”

Imani smiled. The seminar included mathematicians, Assyriologists, statisticians, and one historian who seemed personally offended by every number. They saw only transformed sequences.

No Antarctica. No archive. No gods.

The critics attacked assumptions one by one.

Base choice. Normalization. Selection effects. Copying bias. Missing values. Retrospective fitting. Imani wrote every objection.

At the end, one statistician said:

“If you told me this came from an ancient manuscript, I would call it interesting but underdetermined.”

Another said:

“If you told me it came from an engineered communication system, I would ask what independent prediction it makes.”

Vaziri looked at Imani.

“Good.”

Nico whispered:

“They just repeated your entire methodology.”

“Exactly.”

The important thing was not that experts agreed.

They disagreed in the correct direction.

They asked what evidence would distinguish explanations.

After the seminar, Imani and Vaziri walked outside.

“You are in trouble,” Vaziri said.

“Why?”

“Because this may be real.”

Imani laughed.

“That is not usually what people mean by trouble.”

“It is if your identity depends on being the person who says no.”

Imani stopped. Vaziri looked at her.

“You built armor against credulity.”

“That is called standards.”

“Sometimes.”

Imani did not answer. Vaziri continued.

“If the evidence crosses your threshold, you have to cross with it.”

That was harder than skepticism.

Skepticism protected Imani from embarrassment. Revision required vulnerability. They turned. Cassian opened it.

The Antarctic sequence contained redundant positions that could predict missing values if the correct grouping was used.

If the sexagesimal parse was right, three damaged sections should reconstruct to specific numbers.

If wrong, they would not. Imani leaned over the console.

“Can we verify?”

“Not from the same scan.”

Pike’s voice came from the door.

“Yes, you can.”

No one had heard her enter. She carried a sealed drive.

“SVW has an older image of the same region.”

Cassian looked at her.

“You held it back.”

“Yes.”

Imani smiled without warmth.

“Excellent. A blind test.”

Pike placed the drive on the table.

“The older image is lower resolution but predates the damage visible in your current scan.”

Cassian felt his heartbeat increase. They had a prediction. And an independent earlier state. No mythology could help now. No resemblance. No interpretation.

Either the missing values matched or they did not. Imani looked at Cassian.

“Freeze the prediction.”

He did. Cryptographic hash. Timestamp. No edits. Pike connected the old image.

LOAM was not allowed to see it yet.

A human technician extracted the relevant values according to a preregistered process. First section. Predicted: 12. Observed: 12. Nico stopped breathing. Second. Predicted: 30. Observed: 30. Imani said nothing. Third. Predicted: 6. The technician zoomed. Noise. Ambiguous.

Cassian felt relief and disappointment at once.

Then a second imaging channel resolved the mark. Observed: 6. Nobody spoke.

LOAM received the values only after verification.

CHECKSUM PARSE SUPPORTED.

Imani sat down. Nico whispered, “So.” She looked at him. He thought better of it.

Cassian stared at the three recovered numbers.

For the first time, sixty was not a coincidence they had found after the fact.

Imani made them convert the numbers by hand. Nico objected on principle.

“We have a machine.”

“We have several,” Imani said. “That is the problem.”

She wrote a sexagesimal value on the transparent wall. 1,24,51,10. Nico stared.

“That looks like four separate numbers.”

“In modern notation, yes. In a place-value sexagesimal system, position matters.”

She walked them through powers of sixty, not because Cassian needed the arithmetic, but because she wanted the room to feel how naturally a base system changes what looks simple.

Cassian saw the design advantage immediately.

Sixty tolerated thirds. Quarters. Fifths. Sixths. Tenths. Twelfths.

A civilization dividing cycles, angles, rations, time intervals, or astronomical observations could do a great deal without recurring fractions.

“So it isn’t mystical,” Nico said.

“Correct.”

“It’s convenient.”

“Extremely.”

“That makes it more likely to be reused.”

“Yes.”

Nico smiled. Imani sighed.

“You are learning in the most dangerous possible direction.”

She then showed them Plimpton 322. Not as a magical artifact.

A damaged Old Babylonian mathematical tablet whose surviving table contained large sexagesimal place-value numbers and relationships associated with right triangles. She emphasized every uncertainty.

Its archaeological context was unknown. Its left portion was missing. Scholars had debated what the table was for. Teaching? Problem generation? A broader mathematical exercise? Something else?

“The uncertainty is useful,” Cassian said.

Imani nodded.

“Exactly. Real scholarship does not become weaker when it says we do not know. It becomes bounded.”

LOAM displayed the Antarctic ratio set beside a mathematical abstraction derived from the tablet. Imani stopped it.

“No visual juxtaposition.”

Cassian raised an eyebrow.

“Why?”

“Because human brains will treat side-by-side images as an argument.”

She had him convert both into anonymous matrices instead.

Then she asked LOAM a narrower question:

Do the Antarctic ratios preserve ordering properties under damage in a way comparable to positional mathematical tables? The system processed.

YES, WITH MODERATE CONFIDENCE.

“Moderate,” Imani said. “Good.”

Cassian asked, “What would increase it?”

INDEPENDENT PREDICTION OF MISSING STRUCTURE.

The room went still. Imani looked at the damaged Antarctic sequence.

Then at the fact that part of Plimpton 322 itself was missing.

“No,” she said quietly.

Nico looked delighted and terrified. Cassian understood her concern. A missing section was temptation.

If they reconstructed it and later found whatever they predicted, that would cross from resemblance into something stronger.

Imani said, “We preregister everything.” Cassian nodded.

“Before looking at any additional archive return.”

“Every expected value. Every alternate model. Every failure condition.”

Nico asked, “And if it predicts the next section?”

Imani turned toward the Antarctic feed.

“Then I stop saying no automatically.”

Dr. Laleh Vaziri joined the mathematical review because she expected to kill the hypothesis. She said so immediately.

“I am here as pest control.”

Nico loved her. Imani did not.

Vaziri was a historian of mathematics with a reputation for publicly dismantling sensational claims about ancient numerical texts.

She looked at the Plimpton comparison.

“First problem: modern readers romanticize elegant mathematics.”

Cassian said, “Meaning?”

“We see a numerical table and assume theorem, astronomy, navigation, secret science. Sometimes a table is a table.”

Imani nodded.

“Agreed.”

“Second: sexagesimal convenience is not a fingerprint.”

“Agreed.”

“Third: if LOAM searches enough mathematical transforms, it will find something impressive.”

Cassian opened the preregistration log. Vaziri read it. Her expression changed slightly.

“You limited the transform family before the comparison.”

“Yes.”

“How many candidate operations?”

“Six.”

“Why six?”

Nico began to smile. Cassian said, “Operational categories justified by the damaged archive, not because six is narratively attractive.” Vaziri looked at Nico.

“Good.”

He stopped smiling. She spent three hours trying to break the ratio model.

Changed normalization. Changed column assumptions. Removed one data region at a time. Injected plausible scribal errors. Tested synthetic tables.

The Antarctic pattern weakened under some perturbations. That was reassuring.

Under others it survived. Also reassuring.

A perfect pattern would have been suspicious. Vaziri finally said:

“This is not evidence of Mesopotamian contact.”

Imani said, “Correct.”

“It is not evidence the tablet encodes your archive.”

“Correct.”

“It is not evidence of aliens.”

Nico sighed.

“Correct.”

Vaziri looked at the blinded prediction setup.

“But the relational method is legitimate enough to test prospectively.”

That was the victory, not belief. Permission to continue. Before disconnecting, Vaziri told Imani:

“If this is real, the worst thing you can do is make ancient mathematics look impressive only because aliens touched it.”

Imani answered:

“I know.”

“No. Make sure the book knows.”

Cassian looked at her.

“What book?”

Nico raised a hand.

“No comment.”

It had made a prediction.

And the prediction had been right.
