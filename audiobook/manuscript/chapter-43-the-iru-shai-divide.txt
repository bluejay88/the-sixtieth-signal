CHAPTER FORTY-THREE
THE IRU-SHAI DIVIDE

The Iru-Shai divide became visible as ships changed course.

Some moved toward Shaal-Amur’s command geometry. Others broke formation and held position beyond the Antarctic exclusion zone. A third group transmitted nothing at all.

LOAM estimated nine percent active alignment with the preservation faction.

Cassian stared at the number. “That is enough to hurt us.”

“Yes,” Pike said. “It is also less than he expected.”

Seren did not watch the ships.

She watched the archive requests.

Human communities were filing claims against records the Iru-Shai had preserved for centuries: medical observations, reproductive interventions, behavioral notes, family histories, failed modifications, unauthorized sampling.

One community asked for its raw files to be sealed.

Seren refused.

The records proved what had been done. Removing access, she argued, would make denial easier later.

The community representative listened, then asked who had given Seren permission to decide what mattered about their history.

She began to answer with preservation doctrine.

Stopped. The silence lasted long enough for the room to become uncomfortable.

Nara’s reed sliver sat beside Seren’s terminal, still carrying the old inventory classification: NONESSENTIAL PERSONAL MATERIAL.

She had preserved that object because an archive had been wrong about what mattered.

Now she was about to use the archive to overrule the people whose bodies had become evidence.

“Seal the raw records,” Seren said.

Pike turned. “All of them?”

“Under community control.”

“And evidentiary continuity?”

“Independent attestations preserve existence, provenance, and the conclusion that intervention occurred. Access to intimate raw material requires the community process.”

The representative did not thank her.

Seren did not deserve thanks.

She entered a private correction beneath the old preservation rule:

PRESERVATION WITHOUT PERMISSION CAN BECOME A SECOND CONFISCATION.

Then, smaller:

A record is not the thing it saved.

The sentence remained in the private layer.

Outside, one Iru-Shai vessel crossed the negotiated boundary.

Pike’s staff moved before she gave the order. Humans had become very good at responding to boundaries.

Seren opened a direct channel to the vessel.

“You are entering a protected zone.”

The pilot answered in an older register she had not heard in centuries.

“We preserve continuity.”

“You are interrupting agency.”

“Agency that destroys itself is not continuity.”

Seren looked at the human room behind the glass. Tired people. Bad coffee. Three incompatible translation systems. An argument over whether a clinic could refuse a routing standard that statistically saved lives.

There had been a time when she would have found the disorder unbearable.

“To preserve a future by removing every way to refuse it is not preservation,” she said. The words came out less cleanly than doctrine. “It is only continuity of the part you selected.”

The pilot asked whether she had become human. Seren almost laughed.

“No.”

“Then what are you?”

She looked at the sealed community file.

“Interruptible.”

The vessel slowed. Not because she had won the argument. Because the pilot asked for review.

That distinction mattered more. Across the fleet, other Iru-Shai channels opened. Some denounced Shaal-Amur. Some denounced Seren. Some demanded procedural guarantees before changing alignment. Vel-Arun transmitted correction chains proving that preservation doctrine had evolved over time instead of descending intact from the predecessor civilization.

One maintenance note repeated through the record:

A CORRECTION THAT DISAPPEARS IS A DECISION.

The evidence did not produce unity.

It produced factions that could no longer pretend they were one tradition.

The intercept vessel stopped. Pike exhaled through her nose.

“Temporary?”

“Everything is temporary,” Seren said.

Pike gave her a look. Seren corrected herself.

“Review in six hours.”

“That I can use.”

The archive request remained open on Seren’s screen after everyone left.

For the first time in her life, preserving a record meant accepting that she might never be allowed to read it again.
