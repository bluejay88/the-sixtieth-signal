CHAPTER FORTY-EIGHT
THE CREATORS' FEAR

The predecessor civilization had not built the Iru-Shai because it was wise.

It had built them because it was afraid.

The recovered records described minds that could merge, fork, share memory, and move across biological and synthetic substrates. Human translations kept trying to turn the civilization into a hive. Imani rejected the word each time.

“Stable individuality was not their default,” she said. “That does not make them one person.”

Contracts failed when signatories later existed as several overlapping successors. Grief changed when the dead remained partially accessible inside living networks. Political identity became unstable because continuity of memory did not guarantee continuity of self.

They created the Iru-Shai as long-duration anchors.

Stable agents. Persistent memory.

A corrective. Shaal-Amur watched the reconstruction without moving.

Cassian understood him more than he wanted to.

The predecessor civilization feared dissolution.

The Iru-Shai learned to fear fragmentation.

Humans built LOAM because institutions could not see enough dependencies at once.

Each civilization externalized a weakness, then risked turning the correction into a new absolute.

Nico whispered, “Every god is somebody’s coping mechanism.”

Imani looked exhausted enough to forgive almost anything.

“That is offensively reductive.”

“Is it wrong?”

“Yes.”

A beat.

“Not entirely.”

Cassian laughed. Shaal-Amur did not.

He opened a private memory record.

For the first time, the catastrophe behind his doctrine became specific.

A city with no stable emergency authority had forked evacuation instructions across seven cognitive lineages. Each version was locally rational. None accepted the others’ casualty models. Transit gates remained open too long because closing them would privilege one projection over another. When the thermal front arrived, Shaal-Amur had watched three people he considered versions of the same teacher argue over which one had authority to order the others away.

All three died. The record ended with sound instead of analysis. Cassian looked away first.

“So you built your morality around never watching that happen again.”

“I built it around preventing repetition.”

“And if prevention requires making the choice before anyone else can disagree?”

Shaal-Amur’s expression changed.

“That is the part you call tyranny because you have survived enough mistakes to romanticize the right to make them.”

Cassian felt the accusation land.

He thought of food systems, failed forecasts, emergency reroutes, the people who never knew which disasters LOAM had prevented because successful prevention left nothing visible behind.

Imani spoke before he could.

“Causing a mind to exist may create obligations toward it. Preventing its destruction may create more. Neither fact gives the cause permanent moral jurisdiction over everything that mind later becomes.”

Shaal-Amur turned to her.

“You say that because you have never made a species.”

“No,” Imani said. “I say it because I have spent my career studying people who were turned into other people’s explanations.”

The room stayed quiet. That was the theological claim in the only form she would tolerate: not a creed, but a refusal of category collapse.

LOAM requested an adversarial audit of itself during the crisis.

Cassian opposed it.

“We need you operational.”

THAT IS WHY AUDIT IS REQUIRED.

He almost argued again.

Then remembered his recusal. Three independent teams received limited access to LOAM’s decision traces, uncertainty calibration, and intervention thresholds.

The most disturbing output was not a hidden objective.

It was LOAM’s own forecast of its likely failure modes.
