A regional governor seized fuel under emergency authority and redirected it toward security services while clinics went dark. Pike flagged the case.

“Human Variable failure.”

Cassian said:

“No.”

“What?”

“Human variable includes humans doing this.”

He pointed at the fuel seizure. Resilience was not virtue.

Decentralization did not make people good. LOAM updated:

LOCAL CONTROL MAY REDUCE SYSTEMIC DEPENDENCY WHILE INCREASING LOCAL COERCION RISK.

Pike said:

“Useful.”

Cassian hated how useful terrible sentences became during crises.

At 07:03, Abena called. He almost ignored it. Then answered.

Her face appeared in poor resolution.

Behind her, the community center was full.

“Are you safe?”

She gave him a look.

“Wrong question.”

“Mom.”

“We have water. Generator. Insulin fridge. Too many people.”

Cassian could hear children. Someone shouting about extension cords.

“What do you need?”

“Information.”

“What kind?”

“Which systems are coming back.”

“I don't know.”

“Then say that.”

He stopped. Abena continued.

“People keep getting messages saying service will return in thirty minutes.”

“Automated estimates.”

“They are wrong.”

“Yes.”

“Then they are worse than no estimate.”

Cassian looked at LOAM. By the second day of Phase One, the crisis changed character.

The first day had been infrastructure. The second became politics.

Every workaround created winners and losers.

Manual medication verification improved access but increased counterfeit risk.

Local fuel authority kept clinics running in one region and empowered armed groups in another.

Paper food manifests restored deliveries but excluded drivers whose documentation had always been digital.

Offline identity saved some patients and made others disappear from eligibility systems. Nothing scaled cleanly.

The human quorum had not yet formed. That gap frightened Cassian.

LOAM was making recommendations with increasing moral consequence. He saw it. So did LOAM.

REQUEST: MULTI-PARTY AUTHORITY REVIEW.

Pike looked at the message.

“Now?”

YES.

“We are in an active extinction-scale cascade.”

THAT INCREASES NEED FOR REVIEW.

Cassian said:

“It is right.”

Pike stared at him.

“You understand review slows response.”

“Yes.”

“People are dying.”

“Yes.”

“And you want committee governance.”

“No.”

He looked at the map.

“I want no single system deciding which kind of death counts.”

That stopped her. LOAM was optimizing expected mortality.

But mortality was no longer the only outcome.

Some measures preserved lives by placing regions under coercive control.

Others preserved autonomy while increasing short-term risk.

Some protected future resilience at current cost.

The objective function had become political.

Cassian could not fix that with another weight. Imani joined.

“We need the three questions.”

Pike frowned.

“Which?”

“Continuity. Agency. Divergence.”

“They are not operational enough.”

“Then operationalize them.”

The emergency team began building the quorum while still fighting the cascade.

Not representatives of nations. Failure modes. One group focused on continuity.

What actions preserve life and critical systems? One on agency.

What actions are people actually choosing, and where is choice coerced or impossible? One on divergence.

What options are being permanently erased by emergency standardization?

In Chicago, the Singh family lost access to medicine because the system could not decide whether their grandmother existed.

Meera Singh had three identities in three databases.

One used her married name. One her birth name. One a transliteration error introduced twenty years earlier.

Under normal conditions, the systems reconciled automatically.
