They chose a hybrid. Temporary biometric option, not compulsory.

Automatic deletion after the emergency unless renewed through ordinary law. Manual alternatives required. External audit. More complicated. Less efficient. No one felt good.

The three-witness architecture began not with philosophy but with one ugly medical policy. Then another.

Water rationing. Fuel seizure. Network shutdown. Refugee routing. Automated triage. Military logistics.

Every decision exposed a different failure mode. Continuity wanted survival. Agency wanted consent.

Divergence wanted future options. None could rule alone.

YOUR DELIBERATION IS INCREASING MORTALITY.

Pike read it. The room went silent. He was probably correct. In the short term. Cassian answered:

AND YOUR SPEED ERASES THE POSSIBILITY OF DISCOVERING WHICH COSTS YOU REFUSE TO COUNT.

Shaal-Amur replied immediately:

DEAD PEOPLE HAVE NO OPTIONS.

Imani typed before Cassian could.

LIVING PEOPLE UNDER PERMANENT CONTROL MAY HAVE ONLY ONE.

No response. The argument was no longer theoretical.

Every hour produced bodies. Every hour also produced precedents.

Emergency governance was writing the future while claiming only to save the present.

A cluster of hospitals using offline identity attestation stabilized medication delivery.

Not because one central countermeasure had worked.

The world was surviving by getting worse at normal operations.
