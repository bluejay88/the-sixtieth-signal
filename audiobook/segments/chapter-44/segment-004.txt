During Phase One, the reconciliation service failed. The pharmacy rejected the prescription.

Her granddaughter Priya - not the quorum member, another Priya among millions - argued with an automated service for forty minutes.

Then with a human representative who could see all three records and was not authorized to merge them. Meera needed anticoagulants.

The local clinic had stock. No valid identity path. Priya drove there.

The clinic had moved to manual attestation. Two witnesses required. Priya counted as one.

They needed another person who knew Meera. Her neighbor, Mr. Alvarez, came.

He had known her for eleven years.

The nurse recorded his statement on paper. Meera received the medication.

Then the clinic discovered the paper form had no recognized code for transliteration mismatch.

The nurse wrote it in the margin.

Someone would later have to decide whether that counted.

Across the city, similar improvisations accumulated.

Human beings became temporary adapters between databases that no longer trusted each other. Names. Relationships. Memory.

At an emergency shelter, a father could not prove custody of his daughter because school records were offline. A teacher recognized them.

At another shelter, an abusive partner falsely claimed relation. Manual trust failed.

Staff learned quickly that human testimony was not automatically safer than digital identity. They built layered verification.

Two people. Independent details. School contacts. Paper records if available. Local discretion with written reasoning. Slow. Imperfect.

Better than one broken database or one confident liar.

In Lagos, Temi Adebayo's hospital created a whiteboard listing every medication release made under fallback authority, not to shame staff.

In St. Louis, Phase One turned an apartment building into a clinic by noon.

The building manager, Keisha Rowan, had no medical training beyond first aid.

She had a master key. A paper tenant list. Two battery radios. And the only working elevator because she knew where the manual controller was.

The official emergency map classified the building as residential capacity. That was useless. By 09:00, residents had identified:

three nurses,
one retired respiratory therapist,
two people with insulin requiring refrigeration,
one dialysis patient,
four infants,
seven residents who could not use stairs,
and a teenager whose father repaired generators. Keisha wrote everything on cardboard.

Not because paper was morally superior.

Because the tenant portal was down. One nurse objected.

“Do not put diagnoses on the wall.”

Keisha crossed them out. Privacy. Even now.

They replaced names with apartment numbers and need categories.

REFRIGERATION.
MOBILITY.
MEDICATION.
INFANT.
CHECK-IN.

The building became a local system. Then the problem arrived.

A city emergency team offered evacuation to a regional shelter. The shelter had stable power.

It also had an automated intake system currently rejecting people with identity mismatches. Several residents had mismatches.

One undocumented tenant would almost certainly be flagged. Keisha asked:

“If we go, can everyone enter?”

The emergency worker hesitated.

“I cannot guarantee that.”

“Then we are not all going.”

A nurse objected.

“The dialysis patient needs higher-level care.”

Keisha looked at her.

“So what do we do?”

They split the decision. The dialysis patient went with two witnesses and paper records. Others stayed, not unity. Coordination. At 14:00, the elevator failed.

The generator teenager found the problem.

A control board needed cooling. No replacement fan.

He zip-tied a household fan to the cabinet and ran it through an extension cord.
