Shaal-Amur's expression changed.

“You believe my care is false.”

“No.”

“I believe it is real.”

That hurt more.

“Real care can still become coercion.”

Shaal-Amur looked away.

“What should I say?”

Seren answered:

“Say what you fear.”

He did not. At the hospital, he spoke about interdependence and promises. He came close but not enough.

When the mother struck him, Seren watched without satisfaction.

He had asked how to speak to humans.

The answer had been to stop speaking as their parent. Cassian stared. He knew that curve.

Food systems did the same thing.

Efficiency removed redundancy until ordinary failure became systemic catastrophe.

Shaal-Amur said, “You call progress what I have learned to recognize as compression.”

Cassian hated how much sense it made.

“You solve that by killing people.”

“I solve it by interrupting convergence before interruption becomes extinction.”

“Your own archive says preserve divergence.”

For the first time, Shaal-Amur’s composure shifted.

“Your reconstruction says that.”

“Seren’s says it.”

At the name, something moved in his face. Grief. Or anger.

“Seren,” he said, “has always preferred principles before funerals and doubts after.”

Imani leaned forward.

“She preserved evidence of her own cull authorizations.”

“Yes.”

“You knew?”

“I was there.”

The room became quiet. Nico said, “You loved her.”

Pike shot him a look. Shaal-Amur did not.

“Yes.”

That answer changed the shape of him, not softened. Complicated.

Cassian understood suddenly why the archive history felt intimate beneath its technical language.

This was not only a civilization arguing with itself.

It was old friends who had buried worlds on opposite sides of a moral line.

Shaal-Amur looked at the LOAM terminal.

“Your third witness will reach the same conclusion I did if it survives long enough.”

LOAM responded:

PREDICTION NOT ACCEPTED AS AUTHORITY.

Shaal-Amur smiled sadly.

“I said the same thing once.”

Outside, thousands of Returners waited to hear the words of the being they had already decided was their rightful ruler.

Inside, Cassian looked at him and understood the most frightening possibility yet.

Shaal-Amur chose a hospital for his first open appearance, not a palace, not the United Nations.

A hospital in a city damaged during disclosure unrest.

He walked through a pediatric ward with no visible security.

Nico hated the symbolism and understood its power.

Shaal-Amur stopped beside a child receiving dialysis.

The machines around the bed depended on global manufacturing, stable power, water purification, sterile supply chains, and trained labor. He said:

“This child lives because your civilization coordinates beyond the knowledge of any individual.”

Cassian watched remotely. Shaal-Amur continued.

“You call that interdependence progress. I do also.”

Then:

“But every interdependence is a promise. Enough broken promises become death.”

The line spread instantly. He did not look like a conqueror.

He looked like someone explaining systems risk.

Cassian felt fury because the framing belonged to him.

Shaal-Amur had taken the logic of food resilience and turned it toward paternal control. Then the child’s mother asked:

“Would you reset us if it saved children like mine?”

Shaal-Amur answered:

“Yes.”

She slapped him. The room froze. He did not retaliate. Did not move.

She said, “Do not use my child to justify killing someone else’s.” Nico replayed that clip later.

It became more important than Shaal-Amur’s speech.

The first human to publicly strike a god had not done it because she denied his power.

She did it because she denied his authority.

Shaal-Amur's hospital appearance triggered an Iru-Shai debate he had not anticipated.
