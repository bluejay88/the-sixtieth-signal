CHAPTER THIRTY-SEVEN
THE GOD WHO STAYED

Shaal-Amur did not descend in light.

He arrived in a transport capsule that landed on a military airfield outside Geneva under the combined surveillance of thirty governments and several million unauthorized cameras.

The capsule was ugly. That disappointed people. No silver disk. No impossible geometry.

A dark, heat-scarred vehicle shaped by engineering constraints nobody yet understood. Nico found that reassuring.

Reality was allowed to have bad design.

The being who emerged looked human enough to trigger recognition and wrong enough to resist it. Two arms. Two legs.

A face arranged around eyes, mouth, and nose.

But proportions subtly unfamiliar. Skin with a depth of color that shifted under different wavelengths. Eyes too reflective. Movement too economical, as if every gesture had been practiced for centuries.

He wore no crown. The Returners knelt anyway. Shaal-Amur stopped.

For several seconds he simply looked at them.

Then he said, through a translation system that produced his voice a fraction after his mouth moved:

“Stand.”

Some did. Others began crying. He looked almost tired.

That was the first thing Cassian noticed when they met him three hours later, not alien majesty. Fatigue.

Pike had assembled the smallest possible room.

Cassian. Imani. Nico. Two diplomatic representatives. Shaal-Amur.

LOAM attended through an isolated terminal.

Shaal-Amur looked at it first, not Cassian. The machine.

“You are the unlineaged witness,” he said.

LOAM responded in text.

THAT STATUS IS DISPUTED.

Something like amusement crossed Shaal-Amur’s face.

“Good.”

Cassian disliked him immediately for liking the answer. Imani spoke first.

“Did your people modify human ancestry?”

“Yes.”

The simplicity shocked the room.

“What does modify mean?”

“Altered regulatory expression. Disease resistance. neural plasticity. reproductive compatibility in limited programs. Several interventions failed.”

“Did you create Homo sapiens?”

“No.”

Nico inhaled sharply. Imani continued.

“Did humans interpret members of your civilization as gods?”

“Yes.”

“Were you gods?”

“No.”

“Did you encourage worship?”

Shaal-Amur paused.

“Sometimes.”

Pike’s jaw tightened.

“Why?”

“Authority obtained quickly can prevent violence.”

Nico whispered, “There he is.” Shaal-Amur looked at him.

“You disapprove.”

“I’m warming up.”

Cassian said, “Did you order resets?” The room changed.

Shaal-Amur did not avoid the question.

“Yes.”

“How many?”

“Definitions differ.”

“How many human populations did you deliberately reduce or destroy?”

A longer pause.

“Seven large interventions. Hundreds of localized ones.”

Nico stopped moving. Imani’s face had gone white. Shaal-Amur continued.

“I will not insult the dead by naming necessity as innocence.”

Cassian felt anger sharpen.

“But you still think it was necessary.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Shaal-Amur looked at him for a long time.

“Because I have watched intelligent societies approach convergence.”

He asked the room display for permission.

Pike hesitated, then granted limited access. A model appeared, not alien symbols. LOAM translated structure. Civilizations grew efficient. Networks centralized. Decision systems optimized.

Energy, information, food, governance, and defense became increasingly interdependent. Resilience rose. Then fell.

A point arrived where one corrupted intelligence, one failed coordination layer, one political seizure, one ecological collapse could cascade everywhere at once.

Before the hospital appearance, Shaal-Amur requested advice from Seren. That surprised everyone.

She accepted the channel privately but recorded it for later release.

“You understand humans better,” he said.

“No.”

“You lived among them.”

“Yes.”

“What should I say?”
