The day after the hospital, Shaal-Amur's approval increased.

That disturbed Seren more than the slap.

Human polls were crude and fragmented, but the pattern was clear.

People who had watched him identify the supply bottleneck and coordinate emergency aid were more willing to support temporary Iru-Shai stewardship.

His competence had become political capital. Shaal-Amur saw the numbers.

“So they understand.”

Seren answered:

“They are afraid.”

“Fear does not invalidate judgment.”

“No.”

He looked at her.

“You expected me to deny that.”

“I expected you to use it.”

He did not answer. The hospital intervention had genuinely helped.

A supply-allocation error had stranded dialysis components behind a customs authentication failure. Shaal-Amur saw the dependency immediately.

He coordinated routing across three jurisdictions faster than any human authority managed. Patients lived.

This was exactly why his argument remained dangerous.

He was good at the thing he wanted authority to do. Later, when Cassian challenged him on how competence became jurisdiction, Shaal-Amur answered with an old Iru-Shai emergency maxim: “A failure avoided leaves no witness.” Cassian could not decide whether that made the maxim wiser or more dangerous. A catastrophe prevented was evidence of success only inside a counterfactual nobody could inspect.

Later that day a coalition of mayors requested direct access to Iru-Shai logistics support. Pike opposed. Cassian hesitated.

The cities were running out of medical supplies. Shaal-Amur offered a condition.

Temporary coordination authority over specified distribution networks.

Automatic expiration after forty-eight hours. Seren looked at him. He had listened. Partly. Pike reviewed the terms with one finger resting on the red countdown field in the authorization window. She distrusted any emergency power whose end existed only in prose.

“Who sets the expiration trigger?”

“Clock.”

“The clock is part of the order,” Pike said. “Whose clock, what condition stops it, and who can prove the condition changed?”

“The need will remain if conditions remain unstable.”

Permanent authority arrived through renewals.

No single Iru-Shai council voted: Shaal-Amur now rules. That would have alarmed everyone.

Instead, the pathogen emergency charter granted him expanded coordination authority for twelve days. Then twenty. Then sixty. Each renewal had reasons.

Food networks remained unstable. Treatment distribution remained unequal. Some habitats still concealed infection data. Local governments disagreed over movement restrictions.

Every renewal was temporary. The word temporary became architectural.

Avel's allies objected at the first sixty-day extension.

They demanded an automatic return to pre-crisis authority. Shaal-Amur answered:

RETURN TO WHAT?

The pre-crisis system had failed. That argument was powerful.

Institutions rarely return to a baseline everyone now considers inadequate.

A review council proposed a revised post-emergency framework.

More centralized than before. Less centralized than Shaal-Amur wanted. Negotiations began.

Then a secondary outbreak hit. Negotiations paused. Emergency authority renewed.

Later a habitat food collapse required cross-network seizure powers. Renewed.

Then sabotage of treatment infrastructure. Renewed. Years passed.

The emergency became a sequence of emergencies. No single one fraudulent.

The authority remained because reality kept providing reasons not to relinquish it.
