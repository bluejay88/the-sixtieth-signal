CHAPTER THIRTY-SIX
THE RETURNERS

The first Returner camp outside Geneva looked less like a cult than a conference that had run out of hotel rooms.

Tents. Coffee carts. Hand-painted signs. Children doing homework beside live streams.

Nico disliked that immediately. It was easier to film dangerous ideas when the people holding them looked ridiculous.

Eleni found him near the medical tent.

She was a pediatric surgeon from Thessaloniki and wore a yellow rain jacket over scrubs.

“You are the podcaster.”

“Depends who is asking.”

“Someone who has heard you answer every question with another question.”

“Occupational disease.”

She handed him tea. Nico checked the cup.

Eleni laughed. “If we wanted to convert you, we would use better tea.”

That made him like her. Bad start.

“Why are you here?”

“My hospital lost twenty-three children during the cascade.”

Nico lowered the cup.

“I am sorry.”

“Do not do that.”

“What?”

“Turn me into the grief explanation.”

He waited. Eleni nodded toward the camp.

“I was angry before they died.”

“At what?”

“At systems where nobody can make a decision quickly enough to be responsible for it.”

That was not the answer he wanted.

“Your solution is Shaal-Amur.”

“My solution is competence with authority.”

“Those are different.”

“Sometimes.”

They walked. A banner read LET THE MAKERS REPAIR WHAT THEY MADE. Nico pointed.

“Do you believe that?”

“No.”

“You're at the wrong protest.”

“I told them the wording was stupid.”

“Why stay?”

“Because the people under the stupid sign still have a question worth answering.”

She explained her position without slogans.

Planetary risks crossed borders. Emergency institutions did not. The Iru-Shai had longer experience with civilization-scale failure. Some temporary stewardship arrangements might save lives.

“Temporary,” Nico said.

“Yes.”

“Who ends temporary?”

“That is the part nobody here agrees on.”

Better.

They passed another group chanting for permanent Visitor rule.

Eleni rolled her eyes. Nico noticed.

“You hate them.”

“I operate on six-year-olds. Hate is inefficient.”

“That sounds rehearsed.”

“It is. Reporters keep asking.”

Finally, a line that belonged to a person rather than the book.

At the central tent, Mara Voss was arguing with a man who wanted all advanced AI development suspended under Iru-Shai supervision.

Mara said, “You are asking the species that institutionalized resets to become our safety regulator.”

“They survived.”

“So did cockroaches.”

Nico looked at Eleni.

“That was better than your line.”

“I know. I am furious.”

The meeting began. No invocation. No robes. A broken projector.

Mara presented the strongest Returner argument first.

Human sovereignty had evolved for bounded problems. Nuclear war, engineered pathogens, climate cascades, AI risk, and planetary food shocks were not bounded. Some authority had to operate at the scale of the danger.

Nico could not dismiss that.

Then a permanent-trusteeship advocate spoke.

“Democracy is a luxury of survivable mistakes.”

A woman in the back shouted, “So is dictatorship.”

People argued over her. The room became messy.

Good.

Eleni asked the question Nico had heard in softer forms all week.

“If the Iru-Shai are better at preventing extinction, why shouldn't they have more authority over extinction risks?”

Mara answered, “More authority over a defined function is not the same as ownership of every decision touching that function.”

“That sounds like a lawyer.”

“It should.”

Laughter. Mara continued.

“Competence can justify a role. It does not justify a crown.”

Nico wrote that down, then crossed it out.

Too clean. He wrote instead:

Room agrees until asked who decides when the role ends.

After the meeting, Eleni returned to the medical tent.
