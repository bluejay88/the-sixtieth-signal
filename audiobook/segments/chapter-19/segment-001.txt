CHAPTER NINETEEN
NARA

The first human in the archive had a scar.

That was how Imani knew it was not an icon. Icons simplify.

The figure recovered from the sixth sector had asymmetry.

One shoulder slightly lower. A healed break in the left forearm. Two missing teeth. A line of damaged tissue across the abdomen.

LOAM had not invented the details.

They appeared across three imaging passes.

Arendt reconstructed only what the relational model supported.

Human. Adult. Female with moderate confidence. Age uncertain. Nico stared at the image.

“She looks real.”

Imani said, “Do not confuse detail with biography.” But she sounded unsettled.

Beside the figure appeared two repeated clusters.

SEREN.

And another.

NARA.

Imani hated both labels.

“They are placeholders,” she said.

Nico said, “All names are placeholders until someone answers to them.”

“That is exactly the kind of sentence that gets you banned from peer review.”

Cassian looked at the relational links.

SEREN connected to repair structures, checksum markers, and what might be annotation.

NARA connected to the human figure, biological data, and a repeated environmental sequence.

“What does Nara mean?” he asked.

“Nothing yet.”

“Could it be a name?”

“Yes.”

“Could it be a category?”

“Yes.”

“A population?”

“Yes.”

“An action?”

“Yes.”

Nico said, “Language is terrible.”

Imani said, “Translation is humility with grammar.” Cassian smiled.

Then LOAM identified a temporal relation.

NARA sat before one of the three biological discontinuities, not after. Before.

Arendt said, “That is a problem.”

“Why?”

“If the archive is claiming a human individual predates a genomic intervention, then either we are mapping time wrong, or the figure is symbolic, or the archive is recording intervention history.”

Nico said, “There’s the word.”

Arendt glared at him through the screen.

“Recording. Not proving.”

Cassian pulled up the temporal sector. It did not use dates.

It used ratios between recurring astronomical and environmental events. Imani liked that.

“Absolute calendars die,” she said. “Cycles travel.”

“Can we anchor it?”

“Maybe.”

The archive referenced orbital relations, precession-like cycles, ice layers, magnetic reversals, and something none of them recognized.

Cassian said, “If we can anchor two, we can solve scale.”

Pike authorized access to restricted paleoclimate data.

Arendt added genetic bottleneck ranges. Imani added archaeological horizons.

LOAM fit thousands of models. Most failed. Several survived.

The best placed NARA far earlier than any civilization Nico wanted to discuss.

Hundreds of thousands of years. He looked almost disappointed.

“No Sumer.”

Imani said, “Humans existed before Sumer.”

“I know.”

“You emotionally don’t.”

Cassian focused on the human figure.

“Could be Homo sapiens.”

“Could,” Arendt said.

“Could be another hominin.”

“Yes.”

“Can morphology discriminate?”

“Not from this representation.”

Nico said, “What about DNA?” Arendt shook her head.

“The archive is not handing us a genome file.”

LOAM found a relation between NARA and the four-state chain.

Not enough for full sequence. Enough to infer changes. Three. Each associated with regulatory systems.

Neural development. Immune response. Reproductive timing. Arendt became angry.

“I do not like this.”

Cassian said, “Because it’s wrong?”

“Because it’s plausible.”

That was worse. The archive did not say, WE CREATED HUMANS.

It showed a human figure. A biological baseline. Three modifications. Later descendant patterns.

Cassian felt the seduction of narrative. He resisted.

“Alternative models.”

LOAM generated them.

Medical record. Species comparison. Breeding lineage. Disease progression. Experimental subject. Educational diagram. Imani pointed.
