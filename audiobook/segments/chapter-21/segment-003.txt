Adrian Sol. Maelin Pike. Oceanographer Tomas Venn's predecessor, Dr. Mara Kline. Glaciologist Yusef Hamadi. Systems engineer Elena Cho. Field medic Ruth Varga. Two technicians, Noel Price and Hadi Benyamin.

The first week was ordinary enough to become nostalgia later. They complained about coffee.

Sol lost a glove and accused the continent.

Cho kept stealing everyone's charging cables.

Hamadi sang badly while checking drill pressure.

Pike hated music during work and secretly liked that he ignored her.

The anomaly then was mostly timing drift and acoustic structure. No archive opening. No response.

They drilled a narrow instrument bore.

The equipment returned subglacial resonance patterns that repeated across three sites.

Sol proposed that the repetition was informational. Kline said:

“Or geology.”

Sol answered:

“Geology is information.”

Kline threw a glove at him.

Pike wrote both interpretations into the log.

The team functioned because disagreement was ordinary.

Then Site Four produced the first event nobody could fit.

The acoustic return changed after the team altered pulse interval.

Not simply in amplitude. In timing structure. Cho suspected software. They replaced the controller. Same. Hamadi suspected bore geometry. They moved the emitter. Same. Sol said:

“It is tracking us.”

Pike said:

“No.”

Not because she knew because language mattered. Tracking implied agency. The evidence supported dependence, not intent. Sol accepted the correction, at first. They ran blind sequences.

The return correlated with interval family. Prime. Composite. Randomized. Nothing mystical.

Then a sixty-second loss of telemetry occurred across six sites. All independent systems. Pike ordered shutdown. Sol objected.

“We finally have a coherent anomaly.”

“We have a safety anomaly.”

“No injuries.”

“We lost six telemetry chains simultaneously.”

“That is the experiment.”

“That is a reason to stop the experiment.”

They argued for twenty minutes.

Pike won because field command was hers.

That night, Sol found the thirty-degree rotation relationship. He woke her at 02:13.

She almost refused to open the door.

He held a transparent sheet over a map.

“Look.”

“Adrian.”

“Look.”

The six site vectors aligned after rotational normalization, not perfectly. Enough. Pike stared.

“What did you search?”

“Thirty-degree increments.”

“Why?”

“Because the directional residuals cluster.”

“How many transformations before this?”

Sol hesitated.

“How many?”

“Several.”

“Define several.”

“Twenty-three.”

Pike's excitement died.

“You searched twenty-three transforms.”

“Twenty-three is nothing.”

“It is twenty-three opportunities to find a pretty answer.”

He became angry.

“It survives held-out sites.”

“Which you selected after seeing the pattern?”

Silence. There. The first crack, not fraud. Methodological enthusiasm.

Pike forced a new blind test.

The thirty-degree relationship weakened. Did not vanish. Sol felt vindicated. Pike felt warned. Both were reasonable.

Then the second event happened. No telemetry loss. A synchronization event.

Six independent clocks converged briefly toward the same offset.

Cho thought the time source had been compromised. Impossible.

They physically isolated two systems. The offset recurred. Sol said:

“This is not geophysics.”

Kline answered:

“You do not get to say what it is because you have run out of ordinary words.”

That sentence became the team's unofficial rule.

For another month, they were good.

They attacked every result. They joked. They slept badly. They preserved failures. Then command classification tightened. External analysts arrived. Data access fragmented.

Sol lost direct access to some streams.

Pike defended the restrictions. He took it personally.
