CHAPTER THIRTEEN
A NUMBER THAT REFUSES TEN

Cassian trusted numbers most when they refused to be impressive.

The Antarctic sequence looked terrible in base ten. That pleased him.

It produced awkward fractions, irregular carries, ugly residuals.

In base twelve it improved. In base twenty it improved differently. In base sixty it became boring. Boring was dangerous.

Boring meant the representation might fit the thing rather than force it.

Imani stood beside him while LOAM displayed the same sequence under four number systems.

Nico had named the visualization THE FOUR GOSPELS.

Imani had threatened to throw him out.

“Why sixty?” Cassian asked.

“Because sixty is divisible.”

“That’s the human explanation.”

“Yes.”

“What’s the system explanation?”

Imani looked at him.

“You’re asking whether a nonhuman engineer would independently choose it.”

“Yes.”

“Maybe. If the problem rewards many clean subdivisions.”

“Angles.”

“Time.”

“Synchronization.”

“Allocation.”

“Fractions.”

Cassian thought of food. A warehouse divided among two regions. Three. Four. Five. Six.

A base that made common sharing easy.

LOAM had originally used binary underneath everything, of course. Machines did not need decimal sentiment. But human-facing optimization frequently represented quantities in whichever units minimized operational error.

“Could sixty be a governance number?” Cassian asked.

Imani laughed once.

“That is a very strange sentence.”

“Think about it. If you need systems divided among many coalition sizes—”

“Two, three, four, five, six.”

“Exactly.”

“You’re turning arithmetic into politics.”

“Humans did first.”

She considered.

“Maybe.”

Nico entered carrying three coffees and a printed image.

“Speaking of humans ruining numbers.”

Imani took the coffee but not the bait.

“What is that?”

“King List.”

She closed her eyes.

“No.”

“You haven’t—”

“No.”

Cassian smiled. Nico placed the image down anyway.

The Weld-Blundell Prism, four-sided, densely inscribed.

“I know what it is,” Imani said.

“I know you know.”

“Then why are you showing me?”

“Because LOAM flagged the reign lengths.”

Imani turned to Cassian.

“You let it search the King List?”

“Not for alien matches.”

“Then for what?”

“Normalization tests.”

She set down the coffee.

“Explain carefully.”

Cassian did. They had asked LOAM to search selected ancient corpora for mathematical structures that remained detectable after language was removed.

Not similarity in gods, not floods, not sky beings, not creation stories. Numerical residue.

The Sumerian King List was an obvious stress test because its earliest reign lengths were famously enormous and often treated as mythic.

LOAM normalized the reign values in several ways. Most produced nothing useful.

One produced repeated residue classes under sexagesimal decomposition. Imani’s expression hardened.

“Which version?”

Cassian named it.

“Variant differences?”

“Not yet fully integrated.”

“Then it means nothing.”

“I know.”

“Copying errors?”

“In progress.”

“Modern editorial reconstruction?”

“In progress.”

“Selection bias?”

“Controlled by applying the same method to other royal lists.”

She relaxed slightly. Nico said, “Do you interrogate everyone like this?”

“Yes.”

“I’m starting to understand academia.”

Imani pulled the King List data closer.

“Before anyone gets excited: this is not a technical document.”

Cassian nodded.

“It is ideological. It organizes kingship. It puts cities and rulers into a sequence, includes impossible reign lengths, and crosses a Flood boundary. Different copies differ. It is not a pristine timestamp from prehistory.”

Nico said, “But the impossible ages—”

“Are not proof of aliens.”

“I know.”

“Say it.”

“The impossible ages are not proof of aliens.”

“Good.”
