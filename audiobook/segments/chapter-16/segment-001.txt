CHAPTER SIXTEEN
THE FIELD OF THREE GRAVES

The bodies had not been found under Antarctica. Cassian was grateful for that.

Three ancient human burials beneath the ice would have turned every careful uncertainty into a headline before anyone finished measuring a bone.

The graves were in Central Asia.

A dry valley. A cave system. Excavated years earlier under an unrelated paleogenomics project. Pike projected the site plan.

Three individuals from the same broad period. Different burial positions. No obvious elite goods. No inscriptions. No geometry that screamed ritual to outsiders.

Imani read the archaeological report in silence.

“What did SVW want with these?”

Pike said, “Nothing at first.”

“That is the only answer I like.”

“The genetics team flagged an unusual cluster.”

Cassian asked, “Mutation?”

“Not singular.”

Pike pulled up three genomic analyses.

Cassian was not a geneticist. He knew enough to distrust summaries that collapsed population history into arrows.

Imani knew less technically but more archaeologically.

“Where is the ancient DNA specialist?”

“Joining remotely.”

A woman appeared on screen.

Dr. Linh Arendt. Population geneticist. No patience.

“Before we begin,” she said, “if anyone uses the phrase ‘alien DNA,’ I disconnect.”

Nico raised both hands.

“Everybody is taking away my best lines.”

Arendt continued.

“These individuals are human. Entirely human. Their genomes sit within human variation.”

Cassian said, “Then what is unusual?”

“Three regions.”

She highlighted them.

“Each shows a combination of derived variants that is difficult to model under the local population history we have.”

“Difficult how?”

“Not impossible. Do not convert ‘poorly explained’ into ‘engineered.’”

Imani smiled faintly. Arendt and Imani were going to like each other. Cassian asked, “Selection?”

“Possible.”
“Introgression?”
“Possible.”
“Sampling?”
“Absolutely.”
“Sequencing artifact?”
“Tested repeatedly.”
“Modern contamination?”
“Low.”
“Unknown population?”
“Very plausible.”

Pike said, “The earlier SVW team called them discontinuities.” Arendt frowned.

“I dislike the word.”

“Why?”

“It implies a clean break. Evolution is messy.”

She enlarged the first region.

“The strange part is not that the variants exist. It is the clustering.”

Cassian asked, “Across the three individuals?”

“Yes. And in different combinations among later populations.”

Imani said, “So not a single event.”

“Correct.”

LOAM watched.

Cassian had restricted it from generating historical narratives. Data only.

Arendt supplied allele frequencies, uncertainty ranges, contamination estimates, archaeological dates, and comparison populations. LOAM ran multiple models.

Population bottleneck. Strong selection. Ghost population introgression. Repeated gene flow. Sequencing bias. Multiple localized interventions, included only because Pike insisted.

The last model fit well. Too well. Cassian said, “Penalize complexity.”

DONE.

The model dropped. Good. Ghost population rose. Selection plus introgression rose. Arendt nodded.

“That’s where I’d stay.”

Pike said, “Now add the timing model.” Arendt stiffened.

“I told you I don’t like that analysis.”

“Why?”

“Because the timing model was derived from the Antarctic archive after the fact.”

Cassian said, “Not this version.”

He explained the preregistered checksum structure. Independent of genetics. Arendt listened.

Did not approve. Did not reject.

“Fine,” she said. “But if you bin dates until they fit sixty, I leave.”

“No binning,” Cassian said.

LOAM compared uncertainty distributions. The archaeological dates were broad. Too broad for precise matching.

Three potential intervention windows emerged only under generous assumptions. Cassian disliked it.

“This is weak.”
