“Objects can enter graves later.”

Cassian watched her perform the same discipline she had applied to numbers. Context before story.

The remains were not excavated fully. Pike authorized minimally invasive imaging, environmental sampling, and tiny genetic extractions because disturbing the chamber could destroy information.

One individual was adolescent. That fact changed the room.

Cassian disliked how quickly “ancient subjects” became a child. Imani disliked it too.

“Do not invent personality,” she said.

But her voice was softer.

The token beside the youngest skeleton contained the same ratio family as the archive checksum, not identical marks, not matching script. Relationship.

LOAM compared the token under blind controls. Positive.

Then the genetic analysis returned an anomaly, not “alien DNA.”.

Cassian nearly shouted at Nico for using the phrase before Nico had even spoken. The anomaly was subtler.

A regulatory region showed a pattern of variation statistically unusual for the estimated population background.

Could be contamination. Could be degradation artifact. Could be sampling bias. Could be something human population genetics already contained and the reference database underrepresented.

Imani insisted every possibility remain open.

Then LOAM asked for permission to compare the region not to Iru-Shai biology—none was available—but to the archive’s nonlinguistic sequencing patterns. Pike said, “No.” Cassian agreed. Imani surprised both of them.

“Why not?”

Pike stared.

“Because that is how we manufacture a connection.”

“Only if the comparison is unconstrained.”

Imani preregistered the test herself.

No base-for-symbol substitution. No arbitrary rotations. No searching thousands of transformations and reporting the best. One narrow structural question:

Did the genetic anomaly contain spacing relationships predicted by the archive’s error-correction grammar? LOAM ran it.

The result was weak. Nico exhaled.

Then LOAM partitioned the region according to known biological regulatory boundaries, a transformation justified independently by genetics. The signal strengthened. Cassian felt his skin tighten.

Imani said nothing for a full minute. Finally:

“This still does not mean engineered human DNA.”

“No,” Cassian said.

“It means one biological pattern and one archive pattern share a constrained structural relationship.”

“Yes.”

“And if we find that relationship in living populations?”

Cassian looked at the adolescent skeleton through the glass.

“Then the graves stop being only archaeology.”

They become inheritance, he thought. He did not say it.

Dr. Ana Quispe refused the phrase genetic anomaly.

She was the ancient-DNA specialist Pike brought into the project.

“Everything in ancient DNA is an anomaly until you understand contamination, damage, reference bias, and sampling.”

Nico started to speak. Quispe pointed at him.

“No alien DNA,” Quispe said. “And if we eventually prove intervention, don't turn inheritance into authorship. A genome can carry the consequence of an act without making the actor the author of the person.”

He closed his mouth. She laid out the problems.

Old samples accumulate characteristic molecular damage.

Reference databases overrepresent some modern populations and underrepresent others.

Population structure can make ordinary variants look rare.

Ancient individuals may belong to lineages with no good modern comparison.

Low coverage creates false confidence. Cassian liked her immediately.

“What would make you accept something as engineered?”

“I would first object to the word engineered.”

“Of course.”

“I would want a pattern difficult to produce through known mutation, recombination, selection, demographic history, contamination, or laboratory process.”

“And then?”
