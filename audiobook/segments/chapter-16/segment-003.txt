Imani nodded immediately. Cassian looked at her. She was pale.

“You okay?”

“No.”

Quispe continued. The altered regions were not a single intelligence gene. There was no such thing.

The changes touched developmental regulation, immune response, neural plasticity, and metabolic pathways in combinations whose later effects were difficult to isolate.

Some variants may have been beneficial. Some neutral.

Some harmful in environments unlike the ones in which they first spread.

The intervention did not create humanity.

It changed populations that were already human. Imani said it aloud.

“Human before. Human after.”

Quispe nodded.

“Yes.”

Cassian thought of the note they had written after midnight.

BIOLOGICAL FINDINGS DO NOT BY THEMSELVES ESTABLISH OWNERSHIP.

He had written the boundary before knowing they would need it. That helped. Barely. LOAM asked:

ESTIMATE FRACTION OF CURRENT HUMAN TRAITS ATTRIBUTABLE TO INTERVENTION?

Quispe stared at the terminal.

“No.”

Cassian almost smiled. LOAM waited. Quispe explained.

“Because traits are not additive credits. You cannot say twelve percent Visitor, eighty-eight percent human. Development is interaction. Population history is interaction. Culture is interaction.”

LOAM updated:

ATTRIBUTION FRACTION NOT WELL-DEFINED.

“Correct.”

Imani sat down.

“The public is going to ask anyway.”

“Then keep saying no.”

Nico arrived ten minutes later with Selene on a secure link.

He read the preliminary statement. Then read it again.

The first public briefing on the genomic findings took place without a stage. Imani insisted.

No dramatic podium. No flags. No image of the Antarctic archive behind them.

Just a long table, six scientists, a public data link, and a screen showing uncertainty intervals.

Cassian sat in the second row.

That had also been Imani's decision.

“You are not a geneticist.”

“I know.”

“You are also the person reporters will try to turn into the architect of the discovery.”

“I know.”

“So sit down.”

He had. Ana Quispe presented first.

She explained what the team had actually found.

Several genomic regions showed patterns inconsistent with known human population history under the models they had tested.

Some appeared to contain edits too coordinated to explain comfortably as ordinary drift.

None established when the changes occurred with perfect precision. None established who made them.

None established that every unusual human trait had been engineered.

Then came the sentence the room had been waiting for.

“We have evidence consistent with targeted modification of an already existing human lineage.”

Noise, not cheering. Intake.

Hundreds of people inhaling at once.

Quispe continued before anyone could interrupt.

“We do not have evidence that humans were created from nothing by the Iru-Shai.”

Imani watched the reporters. Some were already typing.

“We do not have evidence that human intelligence as a whole was inserted by an external species.”

More typing.

“We do not have evidence that language, mathematics, writing, religion, agriculture, kingship, or civilization were gifts from nonhuman actors.”

A reporter raised a hand.

Quispe ignored it until the prepared statement ended. Then the questions began.

“Are humans genetically alien?”

“No.”

“Are we hybrids?”

“That term is too imprecise for what we have found.”

“Did the Iru-Shai make Homo sapiens?”

“No evidence supports that claim.”

“Did they alter Homo sapiens?”

“Some evidence supports targeted modification.”

“Why?”

“Unknown.”

“Could they have made us smarter?”

“Specific cognitive claims are not established.”

A reporter stood without being called.

“Then what did they change?”
