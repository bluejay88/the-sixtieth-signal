CHAPTER THIRTY-FIVE
THE DEATH OF ONE TRUTH

Certainty died unevenly. In some places it barely noticed.

A farmer in Iowa watched the Antarctic hearings while repairing an autonomous seeder and concluded that aliens were probably real but corn remained unforgiving.

A school in Lagos added “nonhuman intelligence claims” to its media-literacy curriculum.

A monastery in Greece extended evening prayer by twenty minutes.

A mosque in Jakarta hosted an astrophysicist.

A secular humanist organization split over whether calling the Visitors “creators” conceded too much language.

An evangelical church in Texas doubled attendance after its pastor preached that a maker inside creation was not the Maker of creation.

A temple in Kyoto put up a sign asking tourists to stop leaving triangular offerings.

Nico collected reactions because reactions were what people mistook for the story.

The real story, he thought, was that no institution reacted as one thing.

Every community fractured internally. Scientists argued over evidence thresholds. Religions argued over theology. Governments argued over sovereignty.

AI researchers argued over whether LOAM’s new governance language indicated consciousness or merely adaptive abstraction.

Conspiracy communities split between those who felt vindicated and those who immediately concluded the official Antarctic evidence was a distraction from the “real” visitors.

There was no disclosure. There were disclosures. Plural. Messy. Contradictory. Human.

Imani appeared before an emergency international academic panel by remote link.

The first question came from a historian of religion.

“Does the archive prove ancient gods were extraterrestrials?”

“No.”

The second came from a geneticist.

“Does it prove directed modification of human DNA?”

“No.”

The third came from a planetary scientist.

“Does it prove a nonhuman technological intelligence operated on Earth before recorded history?”

Imani paused.

“Not yet.”

The word yet traveled around the world in seconds. Nico texted her.

Congratulations. You have accidentally invented hope. She replied: I hate language. Then a new dataset arrived.

Pike’s office had released archived atmospheric samples recovered from sealed Antarctic cores near two SVW sites. The samples contained trace synthetic compounds whose isotopic ratios were difficult to reconcile with known industrial contamination at the estimated layer age, not proof. Another anomaly. Independent labs requested splits.

Meanwhile, the biological fragment recovered from the archive underwent a fourth sequencing analysis. The result remained maddening.

It was chemically compatible with life.

Its coding architecture was not cleanly alien.

Portions resembled terrestrial biochemical constraints because chemistry itself imposed constraints.

Other portions contained regulatory patterns no known lineage used in that arrangement. Cassian read the report.

“Could be engineered terrestrial material.”

“Yes,” the lead biochemist said.

“Modern contamination?”

“Very difficult, not impossible.”

“Ancient engineering?”

“Also difficult, also not impossible.”

“Nonhuman?”

The biochemist sighed.

“That word is doing too much work.”

Cassian liked her immediately. At the same time, Shaal-Amur’s name began appearing publicly. Not from an official release.

From the hidden archive fragments crowds had started reconstructing.

A decentralized group calling itself SixtyFold built tools to align leaked carriers. Most of their output was nonsense. Some was not.

One reconstruction produced a title Imani reluctantly considered plausible:

THE ONE WHO REMAINS WHEN CYCLES FAIL.

Shaal-Amur. Online, the title became The Last God within hours. Pike hated it.

“Do not call him that.”
