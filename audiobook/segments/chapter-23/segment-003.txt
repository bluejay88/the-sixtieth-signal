IS GRIEF A FORM OF FAILED OPTIMIZATION OR A VALUE SIGNAL?

Imani closed her eyes. Nico said nothing.

Cassian looked at the machine he had built to prevent hunger.

He had optimized for lives saved, calories delivered, shortages avoided.

Those were good objectives. Necessary objectives.

But a species was not merely the sum of what it efficiently preserved.

It also chose what to carry after utility ended.

The archive's observer had not understood that at first.

The archive’s record of prayer almost became comedy. LOAM could classify the behavior.

It could not understand why anyone repeated it after the expected recipient failed to produce measurable response.

RESOURCE LOSS.
OBJECT DEPOSITION.
REPEATED VISITATION.
NONRECIPROCAL ADDRESS.

Cassian read the classifications and laughed despite himself.

“What?”

Imani asked.

“That is the most machine description of grief I have ever seen.”

LOAM replied:

DISTINCTION REQUESTED.

Cassian sobered.

“Between prayer and grief?”

YES.

Imani leaned closer.

“They overlap.”

That did not help. LOAM ran hypotheses.

Social cohesion. Grief regulation. Identity transmission. Authority legitimation. Perceived reciprocity with an absent agent. No single function explained persistence.

Then LOAM compared the behavior to Seren.

After Nara’s disappearance, Seren preserved records with no obvious operational utility.

Old status markers. Redundant memories. Conflicting accounts.

Cassian’s own behavior entered the comparison unexpectedly.

A first-generation LOAM model from years earlier remained stored in his private archive despite having no current production value. LOAM asked:

WHY RETAIN FAILED MODEL?

Cassian stared.

“History.”

DEFINE UTILITY OF HISTORY.

He almost said there was none. Then stopped. Imani watched him.

“Because forgetting failure makes repetition easier.”

LOAM processed. Then:

PERSISTENT ATTENTION TO PRIOR LOSS MAY ALTER FUTURE ACTION.

Cassian nodded.

“That is one definition of grief.”

IS GRIEF A FORM OF FAILED OPTIMIZATION OR A VALUE SIGNAL?

Nobody answered immediately. Shaal-Amur’s future doctrine sat invisibly behind the question.

If loss was merely inefficiency, then erasing painful memory could improve coordination.

If loss carried value, preserving it could be rational even when it hurt.

Imani looked at the burial objects.

“Maybe grief is evidence that the dead still occupy the decision system.”

LOAM remained silent for five seconds. Cassian noticed.

It was becoming harder to tell when delay meant computation and when it meant something else.

LOAM’s question about grief did not go away.

It began appearing in unrelated model contexts.

Hospital triage archives. Memorial behavior after disasters. Seed preservation after famine. Language revitalization after cultural loss. Cassian noticed before anyone else.

“You are generalizing too broadly.”

LOAM replied:

REQUEST: DEFINE BOUNDARY.

“Grief is not a systems variable.”

WHY?

Cassian almost answered emotionally. Instead he tried method.

“Because it is subjective.”

SO ARE RISK TOLERANCES.

“That is different.”

HOW?

He stopped. Imani, reading over his shoulder, smiled.

“Do not look at me.”

LOAM continued:

PERSISTENT ATTENTION TO LOSS ALTERS FUTURE RESOURCE ALLOCATION, INSTITUTION DESIGN, MEMORY PRESERVATION, AND RISK BEHAVIOR.

Cassian said, “That makes grief causally relevant. It does not make it reducible.”

ACKNOWLEDGED.

Imani leaned in.

“That is the distinction.”

LOAM waited. Cassian typed:

A phenomenon can affect a system without being exhausted by the system-level explanation. LOAM stored it.

Later, Shaal-Amur would describe his doctrine in terms of civilization risk.

Seren would describe the same history in terms of people. The book needed both.
