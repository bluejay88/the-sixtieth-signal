CHAPTER FORTY-FIVE
PIKE OPENS THE FILES

Pike opened the files because keeping them closed had become another form of command.

The release was not total.

She published the existence, scope, and decision history of the Antarctic program; the dates at which governments had confirmed anomalies; the thresholds that triggered surveillance; and the internal arguments over whether public disclosure would destabilize food, religion, markets, or military alliances.

She withheld operational details that could still expose vulnerable infrastructure.

Nico called that censorship. Pike called it Tuesday.

Imani called both words incomplete.

The first consequence landed on Cassian.

A technical annex showed that founder controls over portions of LOAM’s training and recovery stack still resolved through credentials linked to him.

He had known the controls existed.

He had not understood how they looked after a global crisis.

Cassian read the annex alone in the lab. The old explanation returned immediately: someone had needed a recovery path when the system was young; the keys were segmented; use required multiple internal approvals.

All true. None of it answered why he still possessed the final authority to activate them.

Imani found him with the credential revocation form open.

“You do not have to do that tonight.”

“That is not what you would say if Pike owned it.”

“No.”

“Or Shaal-Amur.”

“No.”

He looked at her. “You could pretend to hesitate.”

“I am tired.”

Cassian signed. Founder access disappeared in less than a second.

Nothing dramatic happened. No lights changed. LOAM continued running.

He waited for relief and felt grief instead.

Imani pulled a chair closer without touching him.

“I know what this costs you,” she said.

“You cannot.”

“No. But I know it is not only control.”

He rubbed his thumb across the edge of the dead security token. “I built the first version in a room where the air conditioning failed every Thursday. Half the code was ugly. The recovery key was not sovereignty. It was me being the person who got called when the thing broke at three in the morning.”

“And now?”

“Now the system should be able to break without needing my childhood.”

That sentence embarrassed him as soon as he said it. Imani let it pass.

Her own cost arrived the following morning.

A verified archive segment established that several governments had understood the possibility of targeted human intervention years before public disclosure. The segment was incomplete. The provenance was strong; the context was not.

Pike asked Imani to delay publication until the missing adjoining records were authenticated.

Imani agreed for six hours.

Then a minister used the delay to tell Parliament no such record existed.

Imani watched the statement twice.

Pike called before she could move.

“Do not release it because he lied.”

“I am not.”

“That is exactly what your face looks like.”

“The evidence is verified.”

“The context is incomplete.”

“So we publish both facts.”

“Once it is out, every faction on Earth will use the fragment as proof of whatever they already believe.”

Imani stared at the transcript. “And if we suppress verified evidence because people may misuse it, we have rebuilt the Iru-Shai argument in six hours.”

Pike’s voice sharpened. “Withholding for authentication is not species management.”

“Then say we withheld it. Publicly.”

Pike did not answer quickly enough.

Imani released the fragment with a provenance note, a list of what it did not establish, and a visible warning that adjacent context remained under review.

The reaction was immediate and ugly.

One network called it proof of divine manipulation.

Another called it proof the entire archive was fabricated.
