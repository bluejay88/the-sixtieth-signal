LOAM mapped the event intervals into the same twelve-by-five partition. Several positions aligned. Cassian’s pulse accelerated.

“Shared pipeline?”

“No,” Pike said. “The 2041 data has never been processed by LOAM.”

“That’s not enough. Same analyst could introduce assumptions now.”

Imani nodded.

“We need independent coding.”

They sent the raw 2041 dataset to three analysts who did not know the Antarctic grid hypothesis. Each was told only to identify statistically significant event partitioning. Results returned hours later.

One found no stable partition. Good. One found twelve-bin periodicity.

One found six and twelve. Mixed. Human, not miraculous.

LOAM recomputed with all analysis choices logged.

The twelve-bin structure remained the best fit, but not the only plausible fit.

Cassian felt more trust because uncertainty survived. Then Imani noticed one asymmetry.

“Column three.”

Cassian enlarged. Across multiple rows, column three carried a repeated terminal pattern.

Column six had another. Column twelve another. Thirty was not a column. Sixty was the whole. 3. 6. 12. 30. 60. Nico said, “Where’s thirty?” Imani pointed to the rows.

“Half-grid.”

Six columns by five rows.

The first checksum prediction should have been satisfying.

Instead it made Imani angry, not because it failed because it worked.

The team had preregistered six candidate transformations. Five were already dead. The surviving model predicted that an unopened archive region would contain a repeated relational marker at a specific structural interval, not a word, not a picture. A relationship.

When the scanner returned, the marker was there. Cassian said nothing.

Nico said something unprintable. Imani left the room.

Cassian found her forty minutes later in the equipment corridor, sitting on a hard case with her boots planted wide and her hands wrapped around a paper cup she had forgotten to drink.

“You okay?”

“No.”

“Good result.”

“I know.”

“That is generally why people become scientists.”

She looked at him.

“Do not be charming.”

“I was not.”

“Worse.”

He sat beside her. The corridor hummed with refrigeration and filtered air. Somewhere behind the wall, the archive systems were processing another scan. Imani stared at the cup.

“When I was fourteen my father took me to a lecture about ancient batteries.”

Cassian waited.

“He loved that stuff. Lost civilizations. forbidden archaeology. People who said scholars were afraid of the truth.”

“He thought that?”

“He thought institutions protected themselves.”

Cassian smiled faintly.

“That part is not entirely false.”

“I know.”

“That makes it harder.”

“Yes.”

She rubbed the cup between her palms.

“The lecturer showed an artifact. Said no one could explain it. Said mainstream archaeology ignored it because admitting the truth would collapse history.”

“What was it?”

“A misrepresented object with a good marketing department.”

Cassian laughed once. Imani did not.

“My father came home glowing. He kept saying, finally, finally someone is willing to ask questions.”

She looked down the corridor.

“And I remember thinking I wanted to be the person who could answer him without humiliating him.”

Cassian said nothing.

“That is partly why I became an archaeologist.”

Not to prove him wrong.

To understand how claims earned the right to become knowledge.

“When he died, I found boxes of notes.”

Cassian looked at her.

“Did you read them?”

“All of them.”

“Why?”

“Because grief makes you do inefficient research.”

He smiled. She finally did too.

“He had underlined every claim that made the world feel bigger. Ancient maps. impossible stones. flood survivors. teachers from the sky.”

“Did any of it hold up?”

“Pieces. Never the pieces he wanted.”

She stared at him.
